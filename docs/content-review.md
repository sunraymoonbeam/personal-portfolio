  sentences beat long ones. Plain words beat impressive ones.

  funny. Not corporate, not breathless.

# Projects, in detail

## Kanta

**Claim**
> A shared camera app for events, with face detection and photo organisation.

**Summary, shown on the projects page**
> Basically Google Drive for photos at an event. Everyone shoots on their own
> phone, the photos land in one place, and face detection helps each guest find
> the shots they are in.
>
> I built the backend with FastAPI, Azure Blob Storage and Azure PostgreSQL,
> deployed on Azure Container Apps. Face clustering runs separately on AWS
> Lambda so a burst of uploads does not block the app.

**Highlights**
> Shared photo collection for events
> Azure storage and PostgreSQL backend
> Clustering job on AWS Lambda

**Article**

### Why I built it

Kanta is a collaborative camera app for events. The idea was to make it easier
for people to put their photos in one place and find the ones they care about.
I wanted sharing to work more like a common photo library than a set of
separate chats and albums.

### The backend

I built the backend with FastAPI. Images live in Azure Blob Storage, and Azure
PostgreSQL stores the metadata we use to find and query them. The application
runs on Azure Container Apps. I used an AWS Lambda job for unsupervised clustering to help
organise the photo collection.

*(A diagram sits here. Its caption:)* The storage split in Kanta's backend. An
architecture summary, not a product screenshot.

Kanta also uses face detection to make a large set of event photos easier to
navigate.

### What I took from it

Building a photo app forced me to think about both sides of a file upload:
where the image bytes live and how a person finds the right image afterwards.
The storage and query model had to support the experience together.

## Echo

**Claim**
> A personal language-model experiment trained on my Telegram history.

**Summary**
> A digital clone of myself, built from five years of my own Telegram history.
> The model was never the hard part. Five years of messages is not a dataset,
> and most of the work went into turning it into one. I fine-tuned LLaMA 3 and
> Gemma 3 with Unsloth, then served the result through a small set of FastAPI
> services. The Telegram data lives on AWS S3.

**Highlights**
> Training data from five years of my Telegram history
> LLaMA 3 and Gemma 3 fine-tuned with Unsloth
> FastAPI services with storage on AWS S3

**Article**

### The experiment

Echo is a personal project: I wanted to see what a model trained on my own
messages could sound like. I used five years of Telegram data to build a
digital clone of my writing style. It is an experiment in personalisation, not
a claim that a model can stand in for a person.

### How I built it

I fine-tuned open-source LLaMA 3 and Gemma 3 models with Unsloth. Its
memory-efficient training path made the experiments practical on the hardware
available to me. I split the application into small services behind a FastAPI
backend and used AWS S3 for the stored Telegram data.

The data has its own limits. A chat export reflects who I was speaking to and
what conversations I kept, so the model can only learn from that sample.

### What I learned

Most of the work turned out to be outside the model itself: cleaning the data,
shaping the training set and deciding how to serve the result. It also made me
think more carefully about what it means to build a personal
system from private conversation data.

**Bullets (verbatim from CV, do not edit)**

**Article**

### What we were trying to make easier

Animating a game character usually involves designing a character, rigging it
and building each movement by hand. At AI Singapore, I worked on a GenAI MVP
for a gaming company that would turn a text prompt into 3D motion. A prompt
such as "a man walking" could produce motion that a developer could bring into
Unity.

### Making the data usable

The motion came from different sources and in different formats. It included
joint positions, movement relationships and velocities. I worked on a modular
pipeline to bring those datasets into one training format. That preparation
mattered as much as the diffusion model we trained with PyTorch.

The model also needed motion clips paired with descriptions. Some clips had
missing or incomplete prompts, so we used a video captioning model to generate
descriptions in the data pipeline. The resulting work improved our
text-to-motion metrics by 20% over baseline, as recorded in my résumé.

### Beyond the model

I helped build the training and deployment pipeline and improved the GitLab
CI/CD checks. Testing and deployment time fell by 80%. I also made onboarding
materials and a small interactive repository for practising Linux and version
control. Explaining those basics to teammates with different backgrounds made
me a clearer engineer.

This project taught me to work from the data backwards. A more capable model
cannot compensate for inconsistent motion files and missing descriptions.

## Research Assistant, National Institute of Education. Oct 2023 to Mar 2024

**Page title**
> Finding who spoke when in classroom recordings

**Summary**
> Developed an ASR and speaker diarisation pipeline for analysing student
> participation across classroom recordings.

**Bullets (verbatim from CV, do not edit)**
> Developed a **speaker diarization system** with professors to measure student
> participation and learning outcomes.
> Implemented an automatic speech recognition (ASR) and diarization pipeline
> with Nvidia's Nemo, fine tuning models with self-annotated transcripts to
> **outperform open-source benchmarks by 30%**.

**Article**

### Listening for participation

At the National Institute of Education, I worked with researchers studying how
students take part in discussions. The recordings covered more than 200 hours.
We needed to know who spoke when, alongside what was said, before the
researchers could examine interruptions and participation patterns.

That is a speaker diarization problem. Classroom audio makes it difficult:
voices overlap, microphones pick up noise and short turns can be hard to assign
to the right person.

### The work behind the transcript

I worked on an automatic speech recognition and diarization pipeline using
NVIDIA NeMo. I also labelled and transcribed recordings for evaluation and
model fine-tuning. My résumé reports performance 30% above the open-source
benchmarks we compared against.

We used audio preprocessing, including beamforming with two microphones, to
make the speech signal clearer. The microphones receive the same voice at
slightly different times. Aligning those signals can reinforce sound from one
direction and reduce noise from others.

I learned how much research depends on careful labels and an evaluation set
that resembles the real recordings. A transcript that looks plausible is not
enough if the speaker assignments are wrong.
