## The role

I joined Tessaract in July 2022 as an intern in the professional services
team. Tessaract sells one cloud platform to law firms: cases, clients,
documents and full accounting, including the trust account a firm keeps for
its clients' money. Professional services is the team that takes a new client
from a signed contract to go-live: requirements, set-up, data migration,
document templates, training and testing.

My part was mostly the data. Each new firm arrived with years of records in an
old legal-practice system or in Excel, and every firm kept its books a little
differently. I moved those records into the firm's own PostgreSQL database
without losing or changing anything, set the firm up on the platform, and
rebuilt its documents. My tools were Python, pandas and SQL, and a lot of
checking. From December 2022 I stayed on as a contractor, as an analyst in the
same team.

## Highlights

### Ledger migration

The biggest job was a large firm's accounts: 18 months of journal entries from
its old system, about 600,000 rows of raw reports. Accounting data has one rule
that makes it checkable. In every journal entry the debits equal the credits,
so each year's totals have to tie before and after the move. And every account
an entry uses has to exist in the firm's new chart of accounts, or the entry
has nowhere to go.

My first run balanced, with two account codes missing. Three days later the
firm sent a fuller export. The clean-up was a script, not a week of hand edits,
so I ran the same script on the new files: 453,672 import lines, 153,317
journal entries, and both years still balanced.

This time, though, 268 account
codes didn't exist in the new chart. Loaded as they were, those lines would
have failed or landed in the wrong accounts across a year and a half of
entries. So the list of codes came first: each one needed a mapping to an
existing account, or a new account, before its lines could load.

### A repeatable migration

Clients, cases, invoices, payments, trust money: every record type went
through the same five steps, so I made them a routine:

1. **Extract** from the old system.
2. **Profile** it: row counts, empty columns, duplicates.
3. **Clean and map** it to the platform's fields.
4. **Load** it into the firm's database.
5. **Post and reconcile** through the platform's API, and tie the totals back.

<Figure src={pipeline} zoom alt="The migration pipeline: extract, profile, clean and map with Python and pandas, load one import sheet per record type into the firm's PostgreSQL database, then post invoices and payments by API and tie totals back. A failed check loops back to the mapping." caption="The migration pipeline. A failed check sends the batch back to the mapping step." />

I was doing the same profiling by hand for every firm, so I wrote a
menu-driven Python tool for it. It loaded a CSV, a whole Excel workbook or the
result of a SQL query, cleaned each table, and wrote a report sheet into the
workbook: row counts, empty columns and duplicates for every table, where the
client's team could see them too.

The invoice import shows the loop at work. The platform's importer rejected
the firm's 765 invoices. I test-imported a single row, read the error, fixed
the cause in the script rather than in the sheet, and tried again. A fix in
the sheet would have been lost with the next export. The causes were small
and exact:

- a field called "Tax Type" that the platform knew as "Tax Treatment";
- a "Receivables" that had to be "Receivable", exactly;
- two tax columns that the database wouldn't accept blank.

The checks also caught problems in the firm's own data: a credit note hidden
among the invoices, one invoice with fees but no tax, and three with tax on a
disbursement of zero. Unpaid and paid invoices then went in as separate
batches, 652 and 112, so that each payment could link to its invoice, and the
totals had to match before and after.

### Data that didn't fit

Not every client was a law firm. One was an investment fund, and its records
didn't fit the platform's model. Its shareholding history used six kinds of
transaction, and each one carried share prices that the platform had nowhere
to put. I could have squeezed the data into the nearest fields, but then the
fund's reports would have been wrong. Instead I added price fields for
ordinary and preference shares, put them on the screens, added the missing
transaction type, and mapped the rest:

| The fund's type | The platform's type |
| --- | --- |
| Investment, Subscription | Issuance |
| Sale of shares | Transfer-Out |
| Purchase of shares | Transfer-In |
| Pref. Redemption | Redemption |
| Distribution | Distribution (added) |

Then I imported the fund's investors and their transactions. An intellectual
property firm had a similar gap: its patents and trade marks needed their own
mapping before they could load.

### Set-up and document templates

Each new firm needed its own company record and three admin users, one each
for the company, professional services and customer success. That used to be
several manual inserts, so I wrote a PL/pgSQL function that creates the
company and all three users in one call.

Each firm also wanted its own documents, looking the way they always had. The
platform filled Word templates from its data, with a tag for each field, loops
for bill lines and conditions for tax. I rebuilt firms' invoices, receipts,
credit notes, trust vouchers and letters this way: about 180 template files
across about 10 law firms, counting versions.

### Onboarding

In my first weeks I learned the platform by automating it. I built two UiPath
bots that ran the flows every client would test: log in and create a client,
then raise a supplier invoice, record a payment and post it. It was also the
quickest way to learn how the platform's records fit together, which I needed
for the migrations.

Later I sat in requirement and training sessions with clients and wrote up
what each one needed. The risks there were seldom code: trust balances and
trust transactions exported on different dates, users who sat in two approval
groups, and who on the client's side had time to test.

## Reflections

### Rigour

An import that succeeds can still be wrong. The firm's ledger balanced on my
first run, and it would have been easy to call it done. The fuller export
three days later balanced too, and it had 268 accounts that didn't exist.
Row counts tell you nothing went missing. Totals tell you nothing changed.
Neither tells you that every entry has somewhere to go.

So in any data I move now, I first write down the rules that must hold before
and after: a total, a balance, a key that must exist. Then I check every one
of them, on every run, and the job is done only when they all pass.

### Validating assumptions early

On one migration, the client held back their real data until close to the
deadline, for confidentiality reasons. We had to guess its format, structure
and quality, and write the scripts and reports against our guesses.

When the
real files came, they were much messier than we expected: inconsistent column
names, formatting problems and plenty of missing values. A lot of our
assumptions went out overnight. We cleaned and revalidated everything in the
last few days, and the surprises caused delays and stress for us and for the
client.

Sensitive data that someone else controls tends to arrive late and messy. Now
I ask for a representative sample at the start,
even an anonymised one, and I build in buffer for the surprises. I write
scripts that expect bad data, and I check my assumptions against real files
as early as I can, so a small problem doesn't turn into a big one at the end.

### Managing expectations

We were onboarding a major law firm, and its accountants weren't happy. They
had workflows they knew well, the switch was a big change for them, and the
launch date was close. It was tempting to say yes to every request to keep
them happy. I'd already seen over-promising happen in a training session, and
saying yes to everything would have caused chaos for the team and made things
worse later.

So I told them plainly which changes we could make before launch, which would
take longer, and why. I also prepared plans and guides for the problems I
expected them to hit: migrated data, reconciling records, missing fields.
Their pain points had answers before they asked.

The onboarding went ahead smoothly. Not every request was in on day one, but
the accountants felt supported and knew what to expect and when. I learned
that a clear "not yet, and here's why" builds more trust than a "yes" we can't
keep.

### Care with production data

As an intern I had write access to law firms' live databases: their clients,
their cases, their trust money. Nobody checked my queries before they ran, so
the checking had to be mine.

The habits I took from it: wrap every write in a transaction, check how many
rows it touched, and only then commit. Passwords and tokens go in environment variables, never in a
notebook. I bring the same rules to the agents I build at Carro: the least
access they need, and an approval before any write.

### Automation

I ran the same profiling checks by hand for each firm before I turned them
into a tool. I also inherited a colleague's script that matched a firm's case
folders to its migrated cases, and rewrote it in Python, with notes in the
notebook for whoever came next.

Repeat work is where mistakes creep in, because the fifth time you do a check
by hand is the time you skip a step. A script does the same thing every time,
and the next person can read it. When I do a task a second time now, I write
the tool before the third.
