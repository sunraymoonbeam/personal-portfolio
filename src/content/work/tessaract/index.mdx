## The project

Tessaract sold one platform to law firms: cases, clients, documents and full
accounting, in the cloud. Every firm got its own PostgreSQL database. I joined
the professional services team, the people who move a new firm onto the
platform, and spent most of my time getting each firm's old data in without
losing or changing anything.

The data came from legacy legal-practice systems and Excel files, and every
firm kept its books a little differently. My tools were Python, pandas and
SQL, and a lot of checking.

## Noteworthy projects

### Moving a law firm's ledger

The biggest job was a large firm's accounts: 18 months of journal entries from
the old system, about 600,000 rows of raw reports. Accounting data has one
rule that makes it checkable. Every entry's debits equal its credits, so the
totals have to tie before and after.

I cleaned the reports into 453,672 import lines, 153,317 journal entries, and
checked each year balanced. The check that mattered most found 268 account
codes in the old books that didn't exist in the firm's new chart of accounts.
Loading first would have scattered errors through a year of entries. Instead
each code got a mapping or a new account, and I ran the whole batch again.

### A migration I could repeat

Clients, cases, invoices, payments, trust money: every record type went
through the same five steps, so I made them a routine. Extract from the old
system, profile it, clean and map it, load it into the firm's database, then
post and reconcile through the platform's API.

<Figure src={pipeline} zoom alt="The migration pipeline: extract, profile, clean and map with Python and pandas, load one import sheet per record type into the firm's PostgreSQL database, then post invoices and payments by API and tie totals back. A failed check loops back to the mapping." caption="Five steps for every record type. A failed check sends the batch back to the mapping." />

I built a small command-line tool for the cleaning step, with a report sheet
of row counts, empty columns and duplicates for every table, because I was
doing the same checks by hand for every firm. The invoice import taught me to
read errors closely: a field called "Tax Type" that the platform knew as "Tax
Treatment", a "Receivables" that should have been "Receivable", tax columns
that couldn't be blank. Paid and unpaid invoices went in separate batches, and
the totals had to match before and after.

### When the data didn't fit the model

Not every client was a law firm. One was an investment fund, and its records
had six kinds of share transactions where the platform had five, plus prices
the platform had nowhere to put. I mapped the types, added the missing fields
and updated the screens, then imported the fund's investors and their
transactions.

### Set-up and templates

Each new firm needed its own company and admin users. I wrote a PL/pgSQL
function that created both in one call. And each firm wanted its own
documents, so I rebuilt their invoices, receipts, credit notes and letters as
Word templates filled from the platform's fields: about 180 template files
across about 10 law firms.

## Reflections

### Check the totals, not just the rows

Row counts tell you nothing went missing. Totals tell you nothing changed.
Debits equal to credits caught problems a row count never would, and I still
look for the invariant in any data I move.

### Ask for the real data early

On one migration the client kept their real data back until close to
delivery, for confidentiality reasons. We'd written the scripts against our
guesses about its format. The real files had inconsistent column names,
missing values and formatting we hadn't planned for, and we had to clean and
revalidate everything in the last few days. Now I ask for a representative
sample at the start, even an anonymised one, and write scripts that expect the
data to be messier than promised.

### Say what you can't do yet

On one onboarding, the firm's accountants were unhappy about leaving the
workflows they knew, and the launch date was close. Saying yes to every
request would have sunk the launch, so I told them plainly what we could
change before launch, what would come later, and why. I also wrote guides for
the problems I expected them to hit, like reconciling records and handling
missing fields. The onboarding went smoothly.

### Production deserves a safety net

Looking at my old SQL now, some of it changed user groups on a live database
with no transaction around it and no backup first. It worked. Today I'd wrap
every write in a transaction, check the count it touched, and only then
commit.
