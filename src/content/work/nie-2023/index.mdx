## What the project was

Researchers wanted transcripts of classroom lessons so they could study how
teachers distribute their attention — who gets asked questions, who gets
interrupted, how long a pause is before the teacher fills it. You cannot study
any of that without knowing **who** is speaking, not just what was said.

That is speaker diarization, and it is much harder than transcription when the
recording is a real room.

## Why the audio was the problem

Benchmark datasets are people taking polite turns into good microphones. A
classroom is one ceiling mic, thirty children, chairs scraping, and two people
talking over each other roughly every ninety seconds. My first pipeline scored
well on the benchmark and fell apart on the first real lesson.

## What I actually did

- Rebuilt the pipeline on **NVIDIA NeMo** instead of the framework we started with.
- Hand-labelled about **40 hours** of real lessons to make an evaluation set that
  looked like the actual problem.
- Tuned segmentation for short overlapping turns, which is where almost all the
  error was concentrated.
- Wrote the tooling the researchers used to correct transcripts themselves,
  because they were going to have to either way.

## What changed

Diarization error rate went from **31% to 19%** on our own set. That is still a
long way from good, and I want to be straight about it: the useful outcome was
an honest evaluation set and a pipeline the team could keep improving, not a
solved problem.

## What I learned

That I would rather spend a week building the measurement than a week guessing.
Every real gain we made came after we could see where the errors actually were.
