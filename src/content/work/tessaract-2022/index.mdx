## What the job was

Tessaract builds software for law firms, which means the data is messy,
regulated, and somebody's actual case. I maintained the Postgres side: schema
migrations, stored procedures in PL/pgSQL, and the reporting queries that the
product ran on.

## The thing I still think about

There was a report that took **forty seconds**. Everyone knew. It had been
forty seconds for over a year, and the accepted wisdom was that it was "a big
query on a lot of data".

It was a missing composite index. Adding it took an afternoon, most of which
was spent being nervous about touching production. The report now takes **under
two seconds**.

Nobody had fixed it because it was nobody's job, and because complaining about
it had become easier than owning it. That lesson has been more useful to me
than anything I learned about Postgres.

## What I actually did day to day

- Wrote and reviewed migrations against 300,000-plus records, on a system where
  losing a row is a legal problem, not a support ticket.
- Moved reporting logic into PL/pgSQL where it belonged, and out of it where it
  did not.
- Took my first on-call rotation, and discovered that most incidents are the
  same three things.
- Wrote the runbook, because I had needed one and there wasn't one.

## Why it still matters

It is the reason I care about tracing now. When I joined, I could not see
anything: no query logs I trusted, no timings, no history. Every debugging
session started with an hour of building visibility I should have already had.
