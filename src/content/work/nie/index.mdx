## Listening for participation

At the National Institute of Education, I worked with researchers studying how students take part in discussions. The recordings covered more than 200 hours. We needed to know who spoke when, alongside what was said, before the researchers could examine interruptions and participation patterns.

That is a speaker diarization problem. Classroom audio makes it difficult: voices overlap, microphones pick up noise and short turns can be hard to assign to the right person.

## The work behind the transcript

I worked on an automatic speech recognition and diarization pipeline using NVIDIA NeMo. I also labelled and transcribed recordings for evaluation and model fine-tuning. My résumé reports performance 30% above the open-source benchmarks we compared against.

We used audio preprocessing, including beamforming with two microphones, to make the speech signal clearer. The microphones receive the same voice at slightly different times. Aligning those signals can reinforce sound from one direction and reduce noise from others.

I learned how much research depends on careful labels and an evaluation set that resembles the real recordings. A transcript that looks plausible is not enough if the speaker assignments are wrong.
