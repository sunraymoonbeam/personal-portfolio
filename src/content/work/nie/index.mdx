## The project

At the National Institute of Education, I worked part-time with professors in
the learning sciences group. They'd recorded students' group discussions in
class and wanted to know how each student took part: who spoke, when, how
often and for how long.

Before any of that, something had to work out who spoke when. That's speaker
diarization, and classroom audio makes it hard. A few microphones pick up the
whole room, students talk over each other, and the quiet ones barely register.
My brief was to review the state of the art, then build a pipeline that turns
a recording into talk time per student.

## Noteworthy projects

### Learning a new field, notebook by notebook

I came from a final-year project on animal sounds and an internship in NLP, so
I started by teaching myself. I wrote a notebook for each stage: finding
speech (voice activity detection), turning each stretch of speech into a voice
fingerprint (speaker embeddings), grouping fingerprints by speaker
(clustering), and scoring the result.

I compared three embedding models, ECAPA-TDNN, Resemblyzer and wav2vec2, and
three ways of clustering, and used silhouette and Calinski-Harabasz scores to
guess how many speakers a recording held. Diarization has to answer that
question too, and in a classroom nobody tells you.

### Choosing a toolkit with a number

There were two serious open-source options, pyannote and NVIDIA NeMo. I built
a pipeline step by step from pyannote's parts and ran NeMo's clustering
diarizer, then scored both on a labelled news clip from the public VoxConverse
set. The score is the diarization error rate: the share of speech that's
missed, falsely detected, or given to the wrong speaker.

<Figure src={der} alt="Diarization error rate on one public clip. pyannote VAD with ECAPA embeddings and spectral clustering: 53.6%, almost all speaker confusion. NeMo with MarbleNet VAD and TitaNet multi-scale embeddings: 29.6%, split between missed speech, false alarms and confusion." caption="Same clip, two pipelines. Most of my pyannote build's error was giving speech to the wrong person." />

NeMo made about half the errors. More useful was where my build failed: it
found the speech fine and then mixed up who was talking. That pointed at the
embeddings and clustering, which is exactly what NeMo does better, with
TitaNet fingerprints taken at several window sizes.

<Figure src={timeline} alt="Speaker turns over 170 seconds for four speakers, human labels above and NeMo's output below. The system matches most turns, and misses part of the fourth speaker's." caption="Who spoke when, on the public clip: the human labels, then NeMo's output." />

### Labelling to measure

On the classroom recordings there was no answer key, so there was no way to
know how good any output was. I set up Gecko, an open-source annotation tool,
on my own machine, loaded NeMo's output, and corrected it turn by turn for
eight sessions, about five hours of audio, tagging noise and cross-talk as I
went.

That gave the project its first accuracy number, and showed where the errors
were. Most were missed speech: quiet students the system never heard. And in
some groups it found fewer speakers than were really there, which is the worst
mistake for a participation study, because a student who isn't counted
doesn't take part at all as far as the data knows.

### Tuning, and when cleaning hurts

Missed speech looked like a threshold problem, so I swept the voice-activity
settings. On the public clip, filling short gaps cut the detection error from
17.2% to 15.0%. On the classroom audio, the same tuning barely moved it.
Thresholds can't recover speech the model scores low.

<Figure src={sweep} alt="Detection error against the minimum gap that gets filled. It starts at 17.2% with no filling and reaches its lowest, 15.0%, at 0.5 seconds." caption="One setting swept on the public clip. Filling gaps under half a second helped most." />

Cleaning the audio wasn't the fix either. I denoised a test clip and ran it
again, and the system merged two speakers into one: the denoiser had removed
the very differences that told their voices apart. With two microphones,
beamforming is the better route. A voice reaches each microphone at a slightly
different time, so delaying one signal by that gap and adding the two makes
the speaker louder and the room quieter.

## Reflections

### Label a little before tuning a lot

Five hours of hand-corrected audio told me more than any amount of tuning
without it. The habit I kept: build a small answer key first, even a rough one, before
touching a setting.

### Count the quiet ones

The usual error rate is weighted by speech, so a quiet student who's missed
entirely costs almost nothing. For a study about participation, that's
backwards. I learned to pick the measure that matches the question, not the
one the toolkit prints.

### Cleaner isn't always better

The denoised clip was cleaner audio and worse input for the model. What a
person hears as quality and what a model needs aren't the same thing, so test
every clean-up step against the score.
