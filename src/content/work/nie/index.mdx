## The project

The [National Institute of Education](https://www.nie.edu.sg) (NIE) trains
Singapore's teachers and researches how students learn. From October 2023 to
March 2024, alongside my degree at NTU, I worked there part-time for professors
in a learning sciences research group.

The group had recorded students' group discussions in class. They wanted to
know how each student took part: who spoke, when, how often and for how long.
The job description put it as turn-taking, speaking frequency, duration and
pitch for each student, with transcripts to follow.

All of that depends on one step first: working out who spoke when. That's
speaker diarization. It's a version of the
[cocktail party problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocktail_party_effect),
and when people talk over each other it's one of the
[hardest problems in the field](https://www.pyannote.ai/blog/overlapping-speech-and-speaker-separation-the-hardest-problem-in-diarization).
Imagine pouring several drinks into one glass, then being asked which sip came
from which bottle.

To make it harder, the data was far from clean. The microphones picked up the
whole room, students talked over each other, and quiet students barely
registered. My job was to process the recordings so that each student's speech
could be traced: who spoke when, and for how long. I started by reviewing the
state of the art, then built a pipeline that turns a recording into talk time
per student.

## Highlights

### Voice data format

Each recording is one group's discussion, saved as a single WAV file. The
models I used expect 16 kHz mono audio, so that's the format everything was
converted to.

A diarization system doesn't output text. It outputs turns, and the standard
format for them is RTTM, one line per turn:

```
SPEAKER aepyx 1 2.500 3.660 <NA> <NA> spk00 <NA> <NA>
```

That line says speaker `spk00` starts talking 2.5 seconds into the file
`aepyx` and talks for 3.66 seconds. Everything downstream, from scoring to
talk time per student, is arithmetic on lines like this.

### Voice data processing

The data had a lot of quality problems, and two of them shaped the work.

**Background noise.** I tried speech enhancement first, with a deep-learning
denoiser. It added artefacts and smoothed away some of what makes one voice
different from another. When I checked the output, the system could no longer
tell two of the speakers apart: the raw clip gave two speakers, the cleaned one
gave one.

The reason is a train-test mismatch. Speaker models learn from real, noisy
speech, so heavily processed audio is something they never saw in training.
[Speaker verification research](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.03601) reports the
same: enhancement artefacts can lower the scores. Enhancement stayed out of the
pipeline.

I also read up on beamforming, which uses the tiny delay between
microphones to focus on one voice. It needs two or more microphones recorded
on separate channels, so it stayed in my reading notes.

**No labels.** There were no transcripts and no speaker labels for any
recording. Without labelled data you can't measure a pipeline, let alone tune
or fine-tune one. So I set up [Gecko](https://github.com/gong-io/gecko), an
open-source annotation tool, on my own machine, so the audio never left it. I
loaded NeMo's output as a draft and corrected it turn by turn: moving
boundaries, splitting and merging turns, fixing speaker labels, and tagging
noise, cross-talk and laughter.

<Figure src={gecko} zoom alt="The Gecko annotation tool: an audio waveform with coloured regions, one colour per speaker, playback buttons, and a panel for labelling segments, including crosstalk and NA tags." caption="Gecko in its own demo: each colour is one speaker. Screenshot from the Gecko repository." />

Correcting a draft is much faster than labelling from scratch. It has a cost:
a boundary that already looks right is easy to accept, so labels made this way
flatter the system a little. I chose speed, and read every score with that in
mind.

### Diarization pipeline

Diarization answers one question, who spoke when, in a series of steps:

1. **Voice activity detection:** find where anyone is speaking at all, and drop
   the silence and noise.
2. **Segmentation:** cut the speech into short, overlapping windows.
3. **Speaker embeddings:** turn each window into a voiceprint, a list of
   numbers that describes what the voice sounds like.
4. **Clustering:** group the windows whose voiceprints are close. Each group is
   one speaker.

To learn the field, I first rebuilt these steps one by one from
[pyannote](https://github.com/pyannote/pyannote-audio)'s parts. For the
classroom recordings I used [NVIDIA NeMo](https://github.com/NVIDIA/NeMo)'s
clustering diarizer, with the settings from its meeting preset:

| Stage | Model or method | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voice activity detection | MarbleNet | Gives a speech probability every 10 ms. Speech starts above 0.9 and ends below 0.5. |
| Segmentation | Six window scales | Cuts speech into overlapping windows, from 3 s down to 0.5 s. |
| Speaker embeddings | TitaNet-L | Turns each window into a 192-number voiceprint. |
| Scale fusion | Weighted sum | Builds a similarity matrix per scale and adds them up. |
| Clustering | NME-SC | Spectral clustering that also estimates how many speakers there are. |

<Figure src={vad} zoom alt="MarbleNet's speech probability over the first 60 seconds of the public clip, with the onset line at 0.9 and the offset line at 0.5. Below, human speech segments and VAD segments. Between 34 and 52 seconds, where speakers overlap, the VAD splits speech into many short pieces." caption="Voice activity detection on the public clip. Where speakers talk over each other, around 34 to 52 seconds, the speech gets broken into pieces." />

The window scales are the part I found most interesting. Think about knowing a
friend's voice on the phone. From a whole sentence, you know who it is; from
one syllable, you might not. So a longer window gives a more reliable
voiceprint. But a long window can hold the end of one person's sentence and the
start of the next person's, and then its voiceprint belongs to neither, and you
can't say exactly when the handover happened. A short window gives precise
timing but a shaky voiceprint.

NeMo doesn't choose one length. It cuts the audio at six lengths, from 3
seconds down to half a second, all lined up on the shortest. For every short
window, it compares voiceprints at each length and adds the scores together,
so each decision gets the reliable voiceprint of the long windows and the
precise timing of the short ones. The meeting preset weights every length
equally.

<Figure src={scales} zoom alt="NeMo's windows for the first 16 seconds of the public clip at six scales: 3 seconds with a 1.5 second shift, down to 0.5 seconds with a 0.25 second shift. The human speaker turns are drawn above for comparison." caption="The six window scales over the first 16 seconds of the public clip, with the number of windows each scale makes over the whole clip." />

Then the system has to decide how many speakers there are, which nobody
tells you in a classroom. NME-SC links every window to the windows that sound
most like it, which gives a graph with one tight group per speaker. The
graph's eigenvalues then show a clear jump after the last real group, and
where that jump falls is the speaker count, up to a cap of eight.

While I was learning, I also compared three other voiceprint models,
ECAPA-TDNN, Resemblyzer and wav2vec2, with three clustering methods, and used
silhouette and Calinski-Harabasz scores to judge how well the clusters
separated.

### pyannote and NeMo compared

The classroom runs already used NeMo. Afterwards, in March 2024, I put my
step-by-step pyannote build and NeMo side by side on the public clip, which
has human labels, to see how far apart they were and why. I scored both with
the diarization error rate: the share of speech that's missed, falsely
detected, or given to the wrong speaker.

<Figure src={der} zoom alt="Diarization error rate on the public clip. My pyannote build: 53.6% in all, of which 3.8 points missed speech, 3.7 false alarm and 46.0 speaker confusion. NeMo: 29.6% in all, of which 8.1 missed, 9.1 false alarm and 12.4 speaker confusion." caption="Diarization error on the public clip, split into its three parts." />

One look at my build's output showed something was wrong: one speaker label
kept switching between the female and the male anchor. The score showed
where. My build found the speech well, but 46 of its 53.6 points were the
wrong speaker. It made one voiceprint for each stretch of speech, and one
stretch ran from 2.4 to 18.6 seconds, across four turns by two people. One
voiceprint can't belong to two people. NeMo's short windows avoid that, which
is most of the difference.

<Figure src={timeline} alt="Speaker turns over 170 seconds for four speakers, human labels above and NeMo's output below. The system matches most turns, and misses part of the fourth speaker's." caption="Human labels and NeMo's output on the public clip." />

### Tuning

For a participation study, missed speech is the worst error. A student the
system doesn't hear doesn't take part, as far as the data knows. Even on the
clean public clip, the speaker who talked least lost the most:

  ]}
  caption="NeMo on the public clip, by speaker. S4 talked least and lost more than half of it, most of it never detected as speech. Most of S2's loss went to another speaker." />

Every stage has settings that change what gets counted as speech and who it
belongs to:

| Stage | Settings | What they change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voice activity detection | Onset and offset thresholds; padding before and after each segment; shortest speech to keep; shortest gap to fill | How eagerly the system starts and stops counting speech, and whether short pauses split a turn |
| Segmentation and embeddings | Window lengths and shifts at each scale; the weight of each scale | The trade-off between reliable voiceprints and precise timing |
| Clustering | Maximum number of speakers, or the true number when it's known; how widely it searches for the best graph | How many speakers it can find, and how easily it splits or merges them |

Missed speech looked like a voice detection problem, so on the public clip I
swept two of those settings: the shortest speech segment to keep, and the
shortest gap to fill. Filling gaps under half a second lowered the detection
error the most. I tuned and tested on the same single clip, so that's the best
case.

<Figure src={sweep} alt="Detection error against the minimum gap that gets filled. It starts at 17.2% with no filling and reaches its lowest, 15.0%, at 0.5 seconds." caption="Detection error as the minimum filled gap grows. Half a second was best on this clip." />

Thresholds can only work with what the model scores. Speech that MarbleNet
gives a low probability stays missed whatever the setting. The bigger gains
are elsewhere: a voice activity model trained on classroom speech, and giving
the system each group's size from the class list instead of asking it to
guess.

## Reflections

### Rigour

My first pyannote build looked wrong as soon as I read its output: one
speaker label kept switching between the female and the male anchor. "Looks
wrong" told me there was a problem, but not where it was, or whether my next
change made it better or worse. The error rate did. It split the problem into
missed speech, false alarms and wrong speakers, and 46 of the 53.6 points
were wrong speakers. That pointed straight at one long segment that held two
people.

**A number I can compare beats an impression.** The same thinking is why I
spent two days correcting the classroom output by hand before I tuned
anything. You can't build good AI models without good data, and you can't
judge them without it either. On any new problem now, I build a small
labelled set first, even a rough one, and only then start changing settings.

### Judgement

The standard error rate counts seconds of speech. On the public clip, the
speaker who talked least lost more than half of what they said, about ten
seconds. In the overall score, that was only 6.8 of the 29.6 points.

For most uses, that weighting is fine. For a study about participation, the
quiet speaker is the one who matters most, and the headline number almost
hides them. So I look at the error per speaker as well as the total. Before I
trust a metric, I ask what decision it will support, and whether it can see
the case that decision depends on.

### Scepticism

Cleaning the audio sounded like an easy win, so I tested it before I added
it. On the raw test clip the system found two speakers. After speech
enhancement it found one. The enhancer had smoothed away some of what made
the two voices different.

What helps a person listen and what helps a model can be different things.
So every pre-processing step has to earn its place in the final score: I run
the pipeline with it and without it, and keep it only if the number improves.
At Carro I treat prompt and parser changes the same way.

### Learning fast

I came to NIE from bioacoustics, my final-year project on animal sounds, and
an NLP internship. Speech was new to me. In my first weeks I rebuilt each
stage of a diarization pipeline in my own notebooks: voice activity
detection, voiceprints, clustering, and the metrics to judge them. About
three months after the first notebook, I was running NeMo on the real
recordings.

My hand-built version lost to NeMo, and that was the most useful part. I
enjoy running into problems, because that's where I learn how a system
really works. Losing showed me why NeMo bothers with windows at six scales.
With any new stack now, I build a small version by hand first, then use the
library.

### Care with data

The recordings were of children. Before I touched them, I took a
human-subjects research course and signed an undertaking to keep the data
confidential. I worked on my own machine, and I ran the annotation tool
locally so the audio never went to a server.

I can describe the work, but the data was never mine to share. That's why
every example on this page uses a public clip.
