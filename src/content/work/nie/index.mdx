## The project

At the National Institute of Education, I worked with professors studying how
students take part in group discussions. They had more than 200 hours of
classroom recordings and wanted to know how speaking patterns, interruptions
and turn-taking related to learning outcomes.

First, someone had to work out who spoke when. That's speaker diarization, and
classroom audio makes it hard: voices overlap, students talk over each other
and the microphones pick up the whole room.

## What I built

I built an ASR and diarization pipeline on NVIDIA NeMo. I transcribed and
labelled recordings myself, for evaluation and for fine-tuning, and the
fine-tuned models beat the open-source baselines by 30%.

To clean up the audio, we used beamforming with a pair of microphones. A voice
reaches the two microphones at slightly different times. Delay one signal by
that gap and add the two together, and sound from the speaker's direction gets
louder while sound from other directions partly cancels out.

## Looking back

Labelling the recordings myself was slow, but it gave us a test set that came
from real classrooms, noise and all. The 30% is measured on that.
