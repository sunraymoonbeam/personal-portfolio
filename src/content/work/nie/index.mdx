## The project

At the National Institute of Education, I worked part-time with professors in
the learning sciences group. They'd recorded students' group discussions in
class and wanted to know how each student took part: who spoke, when, how
often and for how long.

Before any of that, something had to work out who spoke when. That's speaker
diarization, and classroom audio makes it hard. The microphones pick up the
whole room, students talk over each other, and quiet students barely register.
My brief was to review the state of the art, then build a pipeline that turns
a recording into talk time per student.

<Figure src={pipeline} zoom alt="The pipeline: classroom audio, optional speech enhancement, voice activity detection with MarbleNet, speaker embeddings with TitaNet, spectral clustering to pick the number of speakers, an RTTM file of turns, human correction in the Gecko tool, then participation measures: talk time, turns and share per student." caption="From a recording to talk time per student. Dashed steps were explored but not used in the final runs." />

The classroom recordings are of children, so every example on this page uses
a public clip instead: a four-speaker news debate from the
[VoxConverse](https://github.com/joonson/voxconverse) benchmark.

## Highlights

### Voice data format

The study had ten group discussions, about 7.4 hours in all, from two classes.
Each one is a single WAV file: 16 kHz, mono, 16-bit, most of them 45 minutes
long. A group had up to eight speakers.

A diarization system doesn't output text. It outputs turns, and the standard
format for them is RTTM, one line per turn:

```
SPEAKER aepyx 1 2.500 3.660 <NA> <NA> spk00 <NA> <NA>
```

That line says speaker `spk00` starts talking 2.5 seconds into the file
`aepyx` and talks for 3.66 seconds. Everything downstream, from scoring to
talk time per student, is arithmetic on lines like this.

### Voice data processing

The audio needed little cleaning: downmix to one channel, and resample to 16
kHz for the models that expect it. The harder problem was that nobody knew
the right answer for any recording.

So I made one. I ran [Gecko](https://github.com/gong-io/gecko), an open-source
annotation tool, on my own machine, loaded NeMo's output as a draft, and
corrected it turn by turn: moving boundaries, splitting and merging turns,
fixing speaker labels. That covered eight sessions, 5.3 hours of recordings
holding 2.2 hours of speech.

I also tried cleaning the audio. I ran one test clip through a speech
enhancement model and diarized it again, and the system merged two speakers
into one. The enhancer had removed some of what made their voices different.

### Diarization pipeline

Diarization is a chain of smaller models. I first rebuilt it step by step
from [pyannote](https://github.com/pyannote/pyannote-audio)'s parts, then
used [NVIDIA NeMo](https://github.com/NVIDIA/NeMo)'s clustering diarizer.
NeMo's version, with the settings from its meeting preset:

| Stage | Model or method | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voice activity detection | MarbleNet | Gives a speech probability every 10 ms. Speech starts above 0.9 and ends below 0.5. |
| Segmentation | Six window scales | Cuts speech into overlapping windows, from 3 s down to 0.5 s. |
| Speaker embeddings | TitaNet-L | Turns each window into a 192-number voiceprint. |
| Scale fusion | Weighted sum | Builds a similarity matrix per scale and adds them up. |
| Clustering | NME-SC | Spectral clustering that also estimates how many speakers there are. |

<Figure src={vad} zoom alt="MarbleNet's speech probability over the first 60 seconds of the public clip, with the onset line at 0.9 and the offset line at 0.5. Below, human speech segments and VAD segments. Between 34 and 52 seconds, where speakers overlap, the VAD splits speech into many short pieces." caption="Voice activity detection on the public clip. Where speakers talk over each other, around 34 to 52 seconds, the speech gets broken into pieces." />

The window scales are the part I found most interesting. A long window gives
a steady voiceprint but blurs the moment one speaker hands over to another. A
short window catches the change but is noisy. NeMo uses both: it computes
voiceprints at every scale, compares windows at each scale, and adds the
comparisons together. Each scale can have its own weight. We kept them equal.

<Figure src={scales} zoom alt="NeMo's windows for the first 16 seconds of the public clip at six scales: 3 seconds with a 1.5 second shift, down to 0.5 seconds with a 0.25 second shift. The human speaker turns are drawn above for comparison." caption="The six window scales over the first 16 seconds of the public clip, with the number of windows each scale makes over the whole clip." />

The clustering step has to decide how many speakers there are, which nobody
tells you in a classroom. NME-SC keeps only each window's strongest
similarities, then looks for the largest gap in the eigenvalues of the
resulting graph. The position of that gap is the speaker count.

Before settling on TitaNet, I compared three other voiceprint models,
ECAPA-TDNN, Resemblyzer and wav2vec2, with three clustering methods, using
silhouette and Calinski-Harabasz scores to judge the clusters.

### pyannote and NeMo compared

On the labelled public clip, I scored my pyannote build against NeMo with the
diarization error rate: the share of speech that's missed, falsely detected,
or given to the wrong speaker.

| Pipeline | Error rate | Missed | False alarm | Wrong speaker |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| My pyannote build | 53.6% | 3.8% | 3.7% | 46.0% |
| NeMo | **29.6%** | 8.1% | 9.1% | 12.4% |


My build found the speech well and then mixed up who was talking. It made one
voiceprint per speech segment, and some long segments held two speakers.
NeMo's short windows avoid that, which is most of the difference.

<Figure src={timeline} alt="Speaker turns over 170 seconds for four speakers, human labels above and NeMo's output below. The system matches most turns, and misses part of the fourth speaker's." caption="Human labels and NeMo's output on the public clip." />

### Tuning

On the classroom recordings, most errors were missed speech, from quiet
students. In some groups the system also found fewer speakers than were
really there. For a participation study that's the worst mistake: a student
who isn't counted doesn't take part at all, as far as the data knows.

Missed speech looked like a threshold problem, so I swept two settings on the
public clip: the shortest speech segment to keep, and the shortest gap to
fill. Filling gaps under half a second cut the detection error from 17.2% to
15.0%.

<Figure src={sweep} alt="Detection error against the minimum gap that gets filled. It starts at 17.2% with no filling and reaches its lowest, 15.0%, at 0.5 seconds." caption="Detection error as the minimum filled gap grows. Half a second was best." />

Thresholds can only work with what the model scores. Speech that MarbleNet
gives a low probability stays missed whatever the setting, so the next step
would have been better audio, such as a microphone per group, rather than more
tuning.

## Reflections

### Ground truth first

At first I could only judge the output by listening to it. That gave me
nothing I could compare, so I couldn't say whether a change helped.
Correcting eight sessions by hand gave the project its first real number, and
it showed the actual problem, missed quiet speech, which listening would never
have measured. Now I build a small labelled set, even a rough one, before I
change any setting.

### Choosing the right metric

The standard diarization error is weighted by speech time, so a quiet student
who is missed entirely barely moves it. For a study about participation, that
is the student who matters most. I learned to ask what decision a number will
support, and to pick the metric for that, rather than use the one the toolkit
prints.

### Testing every step

Speech enhancement sounded like an obvious improvement, and the clip did sound
cleaner. The model did worse, because the enhancer removed some of the
differences between two voices. What a person hears as quality and what a
model needs aren't the same. I now judge every pre-processing step by the
final score, not by how it sounds.

### Learning by rebuilding

I came from animal sounds and NLP, not speech. Rebuilding the pipeline by
hand, one stage at a time, taught me more than reading papers did. When I
compared toolkits afterwards, I knew which stage was failing and why.
