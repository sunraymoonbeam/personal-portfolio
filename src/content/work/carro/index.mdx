## The role

I joined Carro as an AI engineer in July 2025. Carro is a used-car
marketplace in Singapore, Malaysia and Hong Kong. It buys cars from owners,
sells them to buyers, and services them in its own workshops, and it runs all
of that through its own CRM. Every customer request becomes a ticket there: a
question about a car, an offer to sell one, a workshop booking.

My job is mainly to translate those processes into agentic workflows, so that
our people can spend their time on higher-value work.

## Noteworthy projects

### Ticketing agents

Customers reach Carro through many workflows:

- **Triage:** reading each new ticket and sending it to the right team.
- **Buying a car:** gathering the customer's requirements, checking what's
  available, and booking a viewing.
- **Selling a car:** checking the car against Carro's acceptance criteria,
  then booking an inspection.
- **Workshop:** booking a servicing appointment, or handling a repair
  diagnosis.

These agents aren't simply chatbots that answer questions. We integrate them with
Carro's CRM and internal systems, so they do the work across the customer's
whole journey: they look up inventory, create and update tickets, book and move
slots, and message customers on WhatsApp. We started with two agents. Today
the team runs fifteen customer-facing agents, all of them on the new ticketing
agent platform whose development I led.

Before I joined, the team had tried several tools, from Google's agent builder
to [Dify](https://github.com/langgenius/dify). The agents hallucinated and
didn't hold up, so the team settled on
[Google ADK](https://github.com/google/adk-python), which performed well in
production.

ADK had its own limit: context. A workflow like selling a car has many stages,
and each stage has its own rules. That's too much context for one agent, so
every stage became its own sub-agent, with tools and prompts that developers
wrote in code. Every new workflow meant more developer work. We could only
scale vertically, by making each bot do more, and not horizontally, by adding
workflows cheaply.

When we were asked to build the Malaysian sell workflow, the plan was one bot
for each stage: qualification, scheduling, rescheduling and recovery. I asked
why every workflow needed its own bot. Why not one bot that could do all of
it?

I surveyed the agent frameworks available, weighed the trade-offs of
each, and built three proofs of concept:
[Agno](https://github.com/agno-agi/agno), the
[Claude Agent SDK](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-agent-sdk-python) and
[LangChain Deep Agents](https://github.com/langchain-ai/deepagents). Deep
Agents won, with skills that use progressive disclosure. The agent sees a
short list of what each skill does, and loads the full instructions only when
it needs them. So one general agent can run many workflows, and ops can write
their own policies as skills instead of waiting for us to build a new bot.
Setting up a new workflow went from about two weeks to about two hours.

The one I'm proudest of is the Carro Care bot, for our workshop. It handles the
whole customer journey on WhatsApp: it answers questions, books a servicing
slot, reschedules or cancels it later, and keeps our systems and the ops team
in step.

### Department agents

Internal teams wanted agents too, for their own workflows: investigating
tickets, pulling data, putting reports together. We started from
[Nanobot](https://github.com/HKUDS/nanobot), an open-source personal agent,
and changed it to suit a company. It was built for one trusted person, so it
had little security. These are the features I added:

| Feature | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Credential proxy | The agent never holds a key. A proxy adds credentials to its requests, so a jailbroken agent has no secrets to leak. |
| Guardrails | Six layers against prompt injection and data leaks. Cheap pattern filters run first, and an LLM judge checks what gets past them. |
| Lark integration | 47 native tools for Lark docs, Base, calendars and approvals, so the agent works where Carro's staff already work. |
| A2A | Agents can call each other to get the data they need, using the open Agent2Agent protocol. |

Even with these, Nanobot was still a personal bot. When a whole team shared
it, memory and context ran together, and it couldn't tell who was allowed to
do what. So my team built department agents instead. Each one mirrors a real
department: an orchestrator at the top, clusters of related work under it, and
specialists under those. Role-based access control decides which people can
use which agents, tools and skills.

My part is how these agents are changed safely. A business user proposes a
change to a skill, tool, SOP or secret, and an admin approves it before any
agent sees it. I also built the workflow planner, which turns a department's
SOP into steps across its agents, and an assistant that suggests changes to
an agent.

### Harness and cost optimisations

Agents get expensive fast at our volume, especially in Malaysia, our largest
market, with about three in four tickets. Volume is only part of it. The sell
and buy workflows ran on Claude Sonnet and cost far more per ticket than the
rest:

normal ones. When I broke the triage agent's spend down by session, the top
1% took 19% of the cost:

In those sessions the agent went round in circles, hunting for answers it was
never given, such as where a shortened ad link led.

The bigger question was the model. Almost every workflow ran on Claude Sonnet,
chosen at the start as a safe default. We wanted the best mix of quality,
reliability, latency and cost for each workflow, and three things stood in the
way:

- We didn't understand model behaviour well enough to say which model suits
  which workflow, or where a model breaks.
- Our agents weren't model-independent. Each provider caches, batches and
  calls tools differently, so a swap changed both behaviour and cost.
- We didn't evaluate our workflows well, so every model choice was a guess.

So the question became: how do we pick the best model for each workflow, and
swap safely when a better one appears? We started with the harness, the code
around the model, so that every model would behave in the same way:

| Change | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Remove risky tools | No grep, glob, sub-agents or file deletes. Ticket workflows don't need them, and they caused most of the loops. |
| Lock the filesystem | Each agent reads its skills and writes only its own memory, never another customer's ticket. |
| Cap the loops | Soft limits on tool and model calls, and a reminder when the agent calls the same tool again and again. |
| Keep the prompt cache warm | A clock in the system prompt changed every minute, so the cache never held. The fix cut cache writes by 84% in testing. |

<Figure src={repeat} zoom alt="A Langfuse trace. On the left, the same appointment-slot tool is called five times in a row. On the right, the harness adds a message: you have called the same tool several times in a row, try a different approach or tool." caption="The same tool called five times in a row, and the reminder the harness adds." />

Then we studied how each model behaves, and gave each one its own harness
profile. Gemini almost never called tools in parallel and didn't plan unless
told to, so its profile adds planning and batching instructions from
[Google's Gemini 3 guide](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/gemini-3), and
a firmer stop when it keeps searching. DeepSeek already batches tool calls,
but it tends to act when it should wait, so its profile spells out when to do
nothing, following
[DeepSeek's own harness](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/deepseek-harness).
Every model also gets the same base instructions:

### Automations

Not everything needs an agent. I treat AI as the last resort: a fixed flow
with clear rules is cheaper, faster and easier to trust.

An automation is only as good as its spec. Before I build one, I pin down:

- the objective, and how we'll know it worked;
- every condition the flow has to handle, including the unhappy ones;
- each data source, its format and how often it changes;
- the settings ops will want to change without me.

For Carro Care I built the outreach flows on n8n, from the first message to a
customer through to follow-up and engagement. They connect data across Lark,
BigQuery, CAPtain and WhatsApp, and I pulled and checked the data myself
before anything went out. Workshop revenue grew 30% after the outreach went
live.

## Reflections

### Attention to detail

Early on, I proposed storing the files users upload, so agents could use them
in their skills and in the console app. It sounded like one line of work. My
senior kept questioning me:

- What problem does it solve, and for whom?
- What fetches the file, from where, and who's allowed to?
- When does it run, and what does each read cost?
- What happens when the file is missing, too large or the wrong type?
- How will we test it, and how will we know it works in production?

I realised that whatever I say as an engineer carries weight. Things don't
happen by magic. Each of those questions hides a decision, and if we don't
make it on purpose, the code makes it for us. So the habit that helps most is
planning. Before I build, I write down what done looks like and which cases
will break it, and I draw the design as an ERD or a sequence diagram. A
diagram can't skip a step the way a sentence can.

### Trade-offs

When I propose something, a colleague will usually ask: what were the
alternatives, and what's the trade-off? There's no perfect solution. An
engineer should be able to defend how they built something: what they chose,
what it costs, and what they gave up.

The cost isn't only today's. A shortcut that ships this week can cost months
later, in maintenance, in lock-in to one provider, or for the next person who
has to change it. Sometimes the shortcut is still right. My job is to see the
bigger picture, weigh what each option costs now and later, say that plainly,
and pick the one that fits where the business is going.

### Informed decisions

I try to make decisions on evidence, not on a hunch or the loudest opinion.
In AI engineering that takes more work than usual, because it isn't like
traditional software engineering. Code does what you write. A model does what it tends to, and a change that fixes one ticket can
break ten others. So much of my work is experiments: a new model, a prompt
change, a new harness module. Each one has to hold across many tickets, and I
follow the same steps every time:

1. Set the objective, and how I'll know I've met it.
2. Get the current context: the traces, the code, the docs.
3. Form a hypothesis.
4. Choose what to change, and hold everything else fixed.
5. Run it.
6. Look at the raw results.
7. Only then, conclude.

When we switched models, I didn't trust one score. I used three judges and
matched time windows, and checked every serious finding by hand.

### Engineering mindset

A good engineer understands the parts of a system and how they behave, not
only which one scores best. My boss put it as racing. A real race engineer
doesn't say "the Lamborghini is the best car". The answer depends on the car,
the road and the conditions, and the engineer understands why.

It's the same in AI engineering. "Anthropic's models perform best" isn't an
answer. The better question is why another model does worse here, and what we
can change so that it does better. He also hates it when I call a model
"cheap": every model has its own habits, and it's on us to build a harness
that works with them.

What we have to understand keeps moving:

- **Prompt engineering.** When I started, the wording of the prompt was all
  the rage.
- **Context engineering.** As models got better, the wording mattered less and
  what we fed the model mattered more: better connectors, better data.
- **Harness engineering.** Now models take on longer and more complex tasks,
  so the work is the loop, the tools, the memory and the limits around the
  model. Does the agent stay on the rails after a run of errors? What happens
  when memory is truncated, or the context is too full? Does the same harness
  work with every model we run, not only the one we tested?

So the mindset I try to keep is curious and attentive: learn how each new
piece behaves, question what we assume, and keep learning as the ground moves.

### Reading the room

Systems aren't the only thing to read. I try to work out what my boss or a
stakeholder really needs, and to notice how people like to work, so I can work
better with them. A manager asking for "a number" usually needs a decision
they can defend. An ops lead asking for "a bot" usually needs fewer repetitive
chats.
