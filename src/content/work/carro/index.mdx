## The role

I joined Carro, a used-car marketplace, as an AI engineer in July 2025. Most of
Carro's work with customers runs through tickets: a question about a car, an
offer to sell one, a workshop booking. Ops teams in Singapore, Malaysia and
Hong Kong work through them every day. My job is to turn their processes into
agents, and to make those agents cheap and safe enough to run at that volume.

## Noteworthy projects

### Ticketing agents

Carro has many workflows, and customers come in through all of them:

- **Buy:** find a car in stock, ask about it, book a viewing.
- **Sell:** get a car qualified, then book an inspection.
- **Workshop:** book a service, then reschedule or cancel it.
- **Triage:** read each new ticket and send it to the right team.

We build integrations that let agents do this work: look up inventory, create
tickets, book slots, send WhatsApp messages. Put together, that's an agent
fleet. We started with two agents. Today the team runs fifteen, and most of
them are built on the new harness whose development I led.

<Figure src={sell} zoom alt="A WhatsApp chat on a staging account. A customer says they want to sell. The agent asks for the plate, make, model, variant, year, transmission and engine size, confirms the details it found, asks for the customer's area, and lists three inspection centres in Kuala Lumpur." caption="Selling a car on WhatsApp: the agent qualifies the car, then finds an inspection centre nearby. Staging, with test data." />

Before I joined, the team had tried several tools, from Google's agent builder
to Dify. The agents hallucinated and didn't hold up, so the team settled on
Google ADK, which performed well in production.

ADK had its own limit: context. A workflow like selling a car has many stages,
and each stage has its own rules. That's too much context for one agent, so
every stage became its own sub-agent, with tools and prompts that developers
wrote in code. Every new workflow meant more developer work, and we couldn't
scale out.

When we were asked to build the Malaysian sell workflow, the plan was one bot
for each stage: qualification, scheduling, rescheduling and recovery. I asked
why every workflow needed its own bot. Why not one bot that could do all of
it?

I reviewed the landscape, weighed the options, and built three proofs of
concept: Agno, the Claude Agent SDK and LangChain Deep Agents. Deep Agents won,
with skills that use progressive disclosure. The agent sees a short list of
what each skill does, and loads the full instructions only when it needs them.
So one general agent can run many workflows, and ops can write their own
policies as skills instead of waiting for us to build a new bot. Setting up a
new workflow went from about two weeks to about two hours.

The one I'm proudest of is the Carro Care bot, for our workshop. It handles the
whole customer journey on WhatsApp: it answers questions, books a servicing
slot, reschedules or cancels it later, and keeps our systems and the ops team
in step.

### Department agents

Internal teams wanted agents too, for their own workflows: investigating
tickets, pulling data, putting reports together. We started from Nanobot, an
open-source personal agent, and changed it to suit a company. It was built for
one trusted person, so it had little security. I added:

- **A credential proxy,** so the agent never holds a key. A jailbroken agent
  has no secrets to leak.
- **Six layers of guardrails** against prompt injection and data leaks, cheap
  filters first and an LLM judge last.
- **Per-user sessions and sender attribution,** so people in one group chat
  stop sharing one memory.
- **47 native Lark tools,** for docs, Base, calendars and approvals.
- **A2A,** so one agent can hand work to another.

Even with these, Nanobot was still a personal bot. When a whole team shared
it, memory and context ran together, and it couldn't tell who was allowed to
do what. So my team built department agents instead. Each one mirrors a real
department: an orchestrator at the top, clusters of related work under it, and
specialists under those. Role-based access control decides which people can
use which agents, tools and skills.

My part is how these agents are changed safely. A business user proposes a
change to a skill, tool, SOP or secret, and an admin approves it before any
agent sees it. I also built the workflow planner, which turns a department's
SOP into steps across its agents, and an assistant that suggests changes to
an agent.

### Harness and cost optimisations

Agents get expensive fast at our volume, especially in Malaysia, our largest
market, with about three in four tickets. And volume isn't the whole story.
The sell and buy workflows ran on Claude Sonnet and cost far more per ticket
than the rest:

Inside one workflow, a few runaway sessions can cost as much as thousands of
normal ones. When I broke the triage agent's spend down by session, the top
1% took 19% of the money:

  caption="The triage agent over 30 days, by session band. The top 1% of sessions took 19% of the cost, and 41% of the input tokens." />

Those runaways were agents hunting for something they were never given, like
a link they couldn't open. They grepped the disk hundreds of times.

The bigger question was the model. Almost every workflow ran on Claude Sonnet,
chosen at the start as a safe default, not because it suited each workflow.
What we wanted was the best mix of quality, reliability, latency and cost for
each one. Three things stood in the way:

- **We didn't understand model behaviour well enough** to say which model
  suits which workflow, or where a model breaks.
- **Our agents weren't model-independent.** Each provider caches, batches and
  calls tools differently, so a swap changed both behaviour and cost.
- **We didn't evaluate our workflows well.** Without that, every model choice
  was a guess.

So the question became: how do we pick the best model for each workflow, and
swap safely when a better one appears? The answer started with the harness,
so that every model behaves the same way:

- **Remove risky tools.** No grep, glob, sub-agents or file deletes. Ticket
  workflows don't need them, and they cause the loops.
- **Lock the filesystem.** Each agent reads its skills and writes only its own
  memory, never another customer's ticket.
- **Tell the model how to work.** Plan before acting, call independent tools
  in parallel, act instead of promising, and ground every answer.

<Figure src={prompts} zoom alt="System prompt text with four headings: Plan before you act, Parallel tool calls, Use your tools to act, and Ground your answers." caption="Prompt additions that every model gets, whatever its provider." />

- **Stop the loops.** Soft caps on tool and model calls, and a reminder when
  the agent calls the same tool again and again.

<Figure src={repeat} zoom alt="A Langfuse trace. On the left, the same appointment-slot tool is called five times in a row. On the right, the harness adds a message: you have called the same tool several times in a row, try a different approach or tool." caption="Five calls to the same tool, then the harness steps in." />

- **Keep the prompt cache warm.** A clock in the system prompt changed every
  minute, so the cache never held. Fixing it cut cache writes by 84% in
  testing.

After the caps and the locked filesystem, runaways over $1 went from 3.11 per
1,000 sessions to none, and the worst session from $10.01 to $0.66.

With a harness we trusted, I ran judged trials in production, model against
model in matched time windows. Six workflows moved from Claude Sonnet to
DeepSeek at 76–80% lower cost per ticket. SOP adherence held at 93%, against
92% before, with no hallucinations in 118 judged replies.

### Automations

Not everything needs an agent. I believe AI should be the last resort: a fixed
flow with clear rules is cheaper, faster and easier to trust, and it often has
more impact.

An automation is only as good as its spec. Before I build one, I pin down:

- the objective, and how we'll know it worked;
- every condition the flow has to handle, including the unhappy ones;
- each data source, its format and how often it changes;
- the configuration ops will want to change without me.

For Carro Care I built the outreach flows on n8n, from the first message to a
customer through to follow-up and engagement. They connect data across Lark,
BigQuery, CAPtain and WhatsApp, and I pulled and checked the data myself
before anything went out. Workshop revenue grew after the outreach went live.

## Reflections

### Attention to detail

As an engineer, everything I say carries weight. Early on, I proposed a
feature in one line: we'll build this. My senior answered with questions:

- What problem does it solve, and for whom?
- What fetches the data, from where, and who's allowed to?
- When does it run, and what does each run cost?
- What happens when the data isn't there?

I couldn't answer most of them. Things don't happen by magic, so every
sentence I say in a meeting has to survive those questions. Here I've been
drilled not to assume, and to test what I believe. The habit that helps most
is drawing. An ERD or a sequence diagram can't skip a step the way a sentence
can, so I draw before I build.

### Trade-offs

There's no perfect solution, only trade-offs. Four bots or one harness, Sonnet
or DeepSeek, ship now or test longer: each answer gives something up. An
engineer should be able to defend how they built something: what they chose,
what it costs, and what they gave up. My job is to see the bigger picture, say
that plainly, and pick the option that fits where the business is going.

### Experiments, not guesses

AI engineering isn't like traditional software engineering. Code does what you
write. A model does what it tends to, and a change that fixes one ticket can
break ten others. So much of my work is experiments: a new model, a prompt
tweak, a new harness module. Each one has to generalise, not just fix the case
I looked at. I follow the same steps every time:

1. Set the objective, and how I'll know I've met it.
2. Get the current context: the traces, the code, the docs.
3. Form a hypothesis.
4. Choose what to change, and hold everything else fixed.
5. Run it.
6. Look at the raw results.
7. Only then, conclude.

### Thoroughness

Before I build something, I ask how we'll test it. What does done look like?
Which cases break it? What happens on the unhappy path, and how will we know
in production? When we switched models, I didn't trust one score. I used three
judges, matched time windows, and checked every serious finding by hand.

### Reading the room

Systems aren't the only thing to read. I try to work out what my boss or a
stakeholder really needs, and to notice how people like to work, so I can work
better with them. A manager asking for "a number" usually needs a decision
they can defend. An ops lead asking for "a bot" usually needs fewer repetitive
chats.

### Engineering mindset

My boss hates it when I call a model "cheap". Every model has its own habits,
and it's on us to build a harness that works with them.

When I started, prompt engineering was all the rage. As models got better,
the wording mattered less and what we fed the model mattered more. Better
connectors and better data made context engineering the real work. Now models
can take on longer and more complex tasks, so the work is the harness: the
loop, the tools, the memory and the limits around the model. Does the agent
stay on the rails after a run of errors? What happens when memory is
truncated, or the context is too full? Does the same harness work well with
every model we run, not only the one we tested?

He put it as racing. A real race engineer doesn't say "the Lamborghini is the
best car". The answer depends on the car, the road and the conditions, and
the engineer understands why. It's the same with models. "Anthropic's models
perform best" isn't an answer. The better question is why another model does
worse here, and what we can change so that it does better.
