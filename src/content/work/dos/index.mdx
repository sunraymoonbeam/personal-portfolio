## The project

In mid-2023 I interned in the Department of Statistics' Digital Transformation
Unit. GPT-3.5 was new, LangChain had just come out, and a new open model
appeared every week. The brief was a question: what can pre-trained language
models do for statistics work, and which ideas deserve more time?

Two things made it hard:

- **Few labels.** Most of the department's text has none, and labelling is slow.
- **Data stays put.** Data can't move freely between divisions, so a prototype
  had to prove itself on public data first.

Over twelve weeks I read through the field, built three prototypes in
Streamlit, each aimed at a real team's problem, and presented what I found in
a deck of about 190 slides, with a roadmap at the end.

## Noteworthy projects

### Text classification

Survey answers have to be coded into fine categories: about 3,000 for
household expenses, 1,023 for industries. A big model with a prompt is slow
and expensive at that scale, and fine-tuning one needs thousands of labels
nobody has.

SetFit needs a handful. It fine-tunes a small sentence transformer on pairs of
examples, same class or different, then trains a plain logistic regression on
the embeddings. I built DOSetFit around it: train from a CSV of labelled
examples, then classify one sentence or a whole file.

<Figure src={setfit} zoom alt="The SetFit pipeline: 32 labelled examples become sentence pairs; phase one fine-tunes a MiniLM sentence transformer with a cosine loss; phase two trains a logistic regression head; then it predicts one sentence or a CSV in batches." caption="SetFit trains in two phases and runs on a laptop." />

Survey data couldn't leave its division, so I tested on a public benchmark,
AG News. With 8 labelled headlines per class, 32 in all, it labelled 7,600
test headlines at 85% accuracy. Sports was easy. Business and Sci/Tech got
mixed up most, which is fair: plenty of headlines are both.

<Figure src={perClass} alt="Accuracy per class: Sports 97.1%, World 85.1%, Sci/Tech 81.7%, Business 76.2%, overall 85.0%." caption="Accuracy per class on 7,600 AG News headlines, from 32 training examples." />

It's also fast. One prediction took 69 ms on my laptop's CPU. The flan-t5
models I tried for the same job took 0.9 to 42.5 seconds. That's one run each,
and a classifier against a text generator, so it shows the order of magnitude,
not a benchmark. For labelling survey text, the order of magnitude was the
point.

<Figure src={latency} alt="One prediction on a laptop CPU, log scale: SetFit 69 ms, flan-t5-small 0.86 s, flan-t5-base 4.1 s, flan-t5-large 12.8 s, flan-t5-xl 42.5 s." caption="One timed run each, on a laptop CPU in 2023." />

### Question answering

A colleague told me a vague question from the public, or one raised at a
high-profile meeting, could take two to three hours to answer properly. The
answers were in the department's own publications, if you knew where to look.

DOSsier reads those publications for you. It splits the PDFs into chunks,
embeds them, and keeps them in a FAISS index. A LangChain agent on GPT-3.5
looks in that index first, then arXiv, then the web, and shows which pages or
links each answer came from. I started from an open-source template and
adapted it to the department's documents.

<Figure src={rag} zoom alt="DOSsier architecture. Indexing: PDF upload, text extraction, cleaning, chunking at 1,000 characters with 200 overlap, OpenAI embeddings into FAISS. Answering: a ReAct agent on GPT-3.5 calls a lookup tool over FAISS, arXiv search, web search or a summariser, and returns the answer with its sources." caption="DOSsier searches the department's documents first, then arXiv, then the web." />

I tested it on Input-Output Tables publications against three answers from
the team's FAQ. The answers were right in substance. One also quoted a number
from a worked example in the source as if it were a fact, which taught me more
than the two good answers did.

### Report drafting

Over lunch with the economists who write the industry reviews, one of them
said: "The hardest part is to come up with a narrative." The numbers were
easy to get. Saying what they meant took the time.

inDOStry Navigator starts there. Pick an industry, upload its tables, and it
plots them and asks a LangChain CSV agent to write the trend in an
economist's voice. You can ask follow-up questions about the tables, or chat
with the outlook PDFs, with a web search for current events. I built it for
three industries, on public SingStat tables.

<Figure src={indostry} zoom alt="inDOStry Navigator flow: pick an industry, upload tables, plot, a CSV agent writes the trend, then ask follow-ups. A PDF chat tab reuses the DOSsier agent with an economist prompt and web search." caption="From tables to a first draft of the narrative." />

### Sharing it with the department

The deck was the main deliverable: a history of NLP, how transformers work,
in-context learning, fine-tuning and LoRA, SetFit, LangChain, and open models.
I ran Vicuna-13B on my MacBook's CPU with llama.cpp, and let an open model
write the conclusion slide.

The roadmap said LLMs are one component among many. Check whether a use case
is worth it first, then build the boring parts:

- a vector database;
- embeddings that run offline;
- retrieval;
- fine-tuning, only where it pays.

## Reflections

### Understanding the users

My best ideas that summer came from the people who would use the tools, not
from model release notes. The report prototype exists because an economist
told me over lunch that the narrative was the hard part. The Q&A prototype
exists because a colleague told me some questions took hours to answer. I now
ask a team what is slow or painful before I decide what to build.

### Choosing the model

Everyone wanted to try the biggest model. For coding survey answers into fixed
categories, a small model trained on 32 examples was hundreds of times faster,
cheaper and good enough. I start with the smallest model that can do the job,
and move up only when it can't.

### Cloud costs

I left a cloud endpoint for a 13-billion-parameter model running for a week,
and the bill was not small. Every experiment I run now has a budget alert and
a plan to shut it down before it starts.

### Prototypes and production

85% on a public benchmark and three good answers make a promising demo, not a
system. The deck said what it would take to test the ideas on real data:
a vector database, embeddings that run offline, retrieval, and fine-tuning
only where it pays. Most
of that is standard now, and LangChain, which many people called bloated that
summer, runs a lot of production systems, including some of the agents I work
on at Carro.
