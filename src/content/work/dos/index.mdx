## The role

I interned at the [Department of Statistics](https://www.singstat.gov.sg)
(DOS) from May to August 2023, in its Digital Transformation Unit. DOS is
Singapore's national statistics office. It runs the country's surveys and
censuses, and publishes the official numbers on SingStat.

GPT-3.5 was new, LangChain was a few months old, and a new open model came out
every week. My brief was open: learn what pre-trained language models can do,
find where they could help DOS, and say which ideas deserve more time. It
listed about a dozen models to look at, from GPT-2 to GPT-J and flan-t5, and a
list of tasks: classification, question answering, summarisation and entity
extraction.

Two limits shaped everything I built:

- **Few labels.** Most of the department's text has no labels, and labelling
  by hand is slow and costly.
- **Data stays put.** The data is confidential and can't move freely between
  divisions, so every prototype had to prove itself on public data first.

I read through the field, then asked two teams what took them the most time,
and built for that. In twelve weeks I built three prototypes in Streamlit and
presented what I found in a deck of about 190 slides, with a roadmap at the
end.

## Noteworthy projects

### Text classification

Every survey answer has to be coded into a fixed category. The Household
Expenditure Survey has about 3,000 expense categories, and the Singapore
Standard Industrial Classification has 1,023 industry codes. Much of that
coding is done by hand.

The brief asked how many labelled examples per class are enough. In 2023 the
obvious answer was to prompt a large model with a few examples. I tried
few-shot prompts on flan-t5, GPT-2 and GPT-J, and read up on fine-tuning and
its cheaper variants such as LoRA. Prompting a large model for every one of
thousands of answers is slow and expensive, and fine-tuning one properly needs
thousands of labels nobody had. So I picked a small specialist model,
[SetFit](https://github.com/huggingface/setfit).

SetFit trains in two phases. First it shows a small sentence-embedding model
pairs of examples, and teaches it to put sentences from the same class close
together and sentences from different classes far apart. A few labelled
examples make many pairs, so a few labels go a long way. Then it trains a plain
logistic regression on those embeddings to pick the label. The model I used,
a multilingual MiniLM with 12 layers, fits easily on a laptop.

<Figure src={setfit} zoom alt="The SetFit pipeline: 32 labelled examples become sentence pairs; phase one fine-tunes a MiniLM sentence transformer with a cosine loss; phase two trains a logistic regression head; then it predicts one sentence or a CSV in batches." caption="SetFit trains in two phases and runs on a laptop." />

I built DOSetFit around it, for people who don't code:

- classify one sentence;
- classify a whole CSV file in batches;
- train a new model by uploading two CSV files of labelled examples.

<Figure src={setfitApp} zoom alt="The DOSetFit app in a browser. A news headline about AMD's dual-core Opteron chips is entered, and the app returns the label Sci/Tech with a probability of 0.89. The sidebar offers a choice of CPU or Apple GPU." caption="DOSetFit classifying a public news headline. I ran the 2023 code and model again on my laptop in 2026 for this screenshot." />

Survey data couldn't leave its division, so I tested on a public benchmark,
AG News. With 8 labelled headlines per class, 32 in all, it labelled 7,600
test headlines at 85% accuracy. Sports was easy: team names and scores give it
away. Business and Sci/Tech got mixed up most. 360 business headlines were
labelled Sci/Tech, and plenty of tech-company news is both.

<Figure src={perClass} alt="Accuracy per class: Sports 97.1%, World 85.1%, Sci/Tech 81.7%, Business 76.2%, overall 85.0%." caption="Accuracy per class on 7,600 AG News headlines, from 32 training examples." />

The mistakes are the interesting part. Here are six test headlines, the label
AG News gives each one, and the model's answer:

| Headline | AG News label | DOSetFit | Probability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AMD Sheds Light on Dual-Core Plans | Sci/Tech | Sci/Tech | 0.89 |
| Strong Server, PC Sales Boost Microsoft Revenue | Sci/Tech | Sci/Tech | 0.71 |
| Insurers Eye Ivan the Terrible | Business | Business | 0.88 |
| Rivera was a corner stone for Ruiz | Sports | Sports | 0.62 |
| Rumours surround Google browser | Business | Sci/Tech | 0.98 |
| Show lights up Paralympics | World | Sports | 0.83 |

Both errors are confident ones. Most readers would call a Google browser tech
news and the Paralympics sport, so you could argue with the benchmark too. For
survey coding, that means a confidence threshold alone won't catch every
mistake, and the label definitions matter as much as the model.

With 3,000
classes instead of 4, there would be many more pairs like Business and
Sci/Tech. In the deck I proposed class weights, more balanced sampling, and a
hierarchy of SetFit models, one for each group of related labels.

It's also fast. One prediction took 69 ms on my laptop's CPU. The flan-t5
models took 0.9 to 42.5 seconds.

<Figure src={latency} alt="One prediction on a laptop CPU, log scale: SetFit 69 ms, flan-t5-small 0.86 s, flan-t5-base 4.1 s, flan-t5-large 12.8 s, flan-t5-xl 42.5 s." caption="One timed run each, on a laptop CPU in 2023. flan-t5 generated text and SetFit classified one sentence, so read it as an order of magnitude." />

The deck also made the case for using both kinds of model together: a large
model generates training data, and the small one does the labelling at
volume.

### Question answering

SingStat gets a steady stream of questions. Vague ones arrive by e-mail from
the public. Hard ones come up at high-profile meetings such as the Committee
of Supply debate, where every answer has to be right. A colleague on the
Input-Output Tables team told me one of those could take two to three hours to
answer. The answers were in the department's own publications, if you knew
where to look.

DOSsier reads those publications for you. I started from an open-source
"chat with your PDF" app and adapted it to the department's documents. It
splits each PDF into chunks of 1,000 characters that overlap by 200, so a
sentence cut at one edge is still whole in the next chunk, and tags each chunk
with its page. It embeds the chunks with OpenAI's embedding model and keeps
them in a FAISS index, which finds the chunks closest in meaning to a
question.

A LangChain agent on GPT-3.5 answers the question. It works in a loop: think,
pick a tool, read the result, and decide again. Its prompt sets the order:
look in the documents first, then arXiv, then the web. The answer comes with
the pages and links it used, because a number quoted at a ministry meeting
has to be checked.

<Figure src={rag} zoom alt="DOSsier architecture. Indexing: PDF upload, text extraction, cleaning, chunking at 1,000 characters with 200 overlap, OpenAI embeddings into FAISS. Answering: a ReAct agent on GPT-3.5 calls a lookup tool over FAISS, arXiv search, web search or a summariser, and returns the answer with its sources." caption="DOSsier searches the department's documents first, then arXiv, then the web." />

I loaded five public Input-Output Tables publications, 32 to 585 pages each,
and asked three questions from the team's FAQ:

| Question | What DOSsier did |
| --- | --- |
| What is the output multiplier? | Gave the right definition, then added that "the output multiplier for Industry A is $1.28 million" |
| What are backward and forward linkages? | Right, and more detailed than the FAQ |
| What is the Domestic Supply Table? | Right, and more detailed than the FAQ |

The $1.28 million came from a worked example in the publication. Industry A
isn't a real industry. The answer was correct for the example, but a reader
could take it as an official figure, and for a statistics office that's worse
than no answer. My prompt asked the model to answer "elaborately", and it did.

Three questions showed the idea could work. A real test would need every FAQ
item and real past questions, scored by the team, and a prompt that cites the
page and says "I don't know" when the documents don't have the answer.

### Report drafting

The economists who write the industry reviews analyse each industry's GDP
every quarter. They read tables, outlooks and other agencies' data. Over lunch,
one of them said: "The hardest part is to come up with a narrative." Plotting
the numbers was the easy part. Saying what they meant took the time.

inDOStry Navigator starts there. Pick an industry from a list of 16 and upload
its tables. It plots each table, then asks a LangChain CSV agent to describe
the trend as an economist in Singapore would. The agent writes pandas code,
runs it on the table and explains the result, so the figures in the text come
from the data and the model doesn't have to guess them.

One agent handles a
single table. For several tables at once I used OpenAI's function calling,
which had come out that June.

<Figure src={indostry} zoom alt="inDOStry Navigator flow: pick an industry, upload tables, plot, a CSV agent writes the trend, then ask follow-ups. A PDF chat tab reuses the DOSsier agent with an economist prompt and web search." caption="From tables to a first draft of the narrative." />

You can then ask follow-up questions about the tables, or chat with the
outlook PDFs through the DOSsier agent, with a web search for current events.
I built it for three industries, on public SingStat tables: infocomm, finance
and insurance, and accommodation.

The agent was good at saying what went up and what went down. The narrative
is the "why", and that still needed the outlook reports and the economist's
judgement. The PDF chat was a first step towards joining the two.

## Reflections

### Listening to users

My best ideas that summer came from the people who'd use the tools. The brief
gave me a list of tasks. The economists gave me one sentence over lunch, and
it told me more: the narrative was the hard part. If I'd built from the brief
alone, I'd have built charts, which they could already make. The Q&A
prototype shows its sources because a colleague told me the hard questions
came from high-profile meetings, where every number gets checked.

The way people describe their problem is often the requirement. "Narrative"
meant the tool had to write prose. "High-profile meeting" meant sources
mattered more than fluent answers. I now ask a team what's slow or painful
before I decide what to build, and I write their answer down in their words.

### Pragmatism

The brief listed a dozen large models. For coding survey answers into fixed
categories, a small model trained on 32 examples answered in 69 ms on a
laptop, where the largest flan-t5 took 42.5 seconds. A classifier picks one
label from a fixed set in one pass. A generator writes its answer one token at
a time and pays for its size on every token, and most of what it knows goes
unused on a fixed task.

In the deck I wrote that smaller specialist models still have a place, and
that large models could make training data for them. I still start with the
smallest model that can do the job, and measure cost per task as well as
accuracy. At Carro, choosing the right model for each workflow is a big part
of how we cut our LLM costs.

### Ownership

To test hosting open models, I deployed LLaMA-13B on an AWS endpoint and left
it running for a week. A GPU endpoint bills for every hour it's up, whether
anyone uses it or not. The fees were high. I put it in my final talk anyway, under the challenges I'd faced, so
the next person wouldn't make the same mistake.

I knew endpoints cost money. The gap was in my process: nothing in my plan
ended the test. Now a teardown step and a budget alert go into the plan before
I deploy anything.

### Explaining clearly

My audience knew statistics far better than I did, but transformers and LLMs
were new to most of them. I enjoy breaking down complex ideas into clear,
practical terms, so I built the deck for them. Every chapter had a one-slide
summary, and the detail went to the annex.

To explain few-shot learning, I
compared three students: a huge model that went to every tuition class, a
smaller one that did all the past-year papers, and you, with only a handful of
examples. With so few examples, you learn faster by comparing them in pairs,
which is what SetFit does.

If I can explain an idea that simply, I understand it. If I can't, I'm not
ready to present it yet.

### Systems thinking

On the roadmap slide I wrote that LLMs are one component among many. Building
the prototypes taught me that. Most of DOSsier's code reads, cleans and
splits PDFs, keeps the page numbers, and shows the sources. The call to
GPT-3.5 is a few lines. Wrong chunks or missing tables break an answer
however good the model is.

Not every call I made aged well. I wrote that open-source models "just aren't
there yet (for now)", and the "for now" did a lot of work. That summer many
people called LangChain bloated. Today it runs a lot of production systems,
including the agents I work on at Carro, where most of our work goes into the
harness around the model.
