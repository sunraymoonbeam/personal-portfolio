## The project

Animating a game character takes a lot of manual work. An artist models the
character, rigs it with a skeleton and controls, then poses each movement by
hand. One character can take weeks.

<Clips caption="Rigging a character by hand: every control on the body is something an animator moves, frame by frame.">
  <Clip wide src="/work/ai-singapore/rig.mp4" poster="/work/ai-singapore/rig.webp"
        alt="A 3D male character in a T-pose inside an animation tool, surrounded by rig controls. The controls move and the leg lifts." />
</Clips>

At AI Singapore I was one of five apprentices on a seven-month project with a
game studio and SUTD. The goal was to generate 3D human motion from text. Type
"a man walking", and the model gives back a walking motion that can drive a
game character. ChatGPT for motion, if you will.

We delivered more than a model. We built a framework to train, evaluate and
serve text-to-motion models, so the studio could retrain and extend it after
we left:

- **Data:** one pipeline that turns motion capture from different datasets
  into one format, versioned with DVC.
- **Training:** T2M and MDM behind one common interface, so a new model plugs
  in without changes to the rest of the code. Hydra configs set up each run,
  and MLflow tracks it.
- **Evaluation:** one module that scores every model with the same metrics,
  during training and after it.
- **Inference:** batch generation, and a FastAPI web interface that turns a
  prompt into an animation, as a stick figure or an SMPL body mesh.
- **Delivery:** Docker images with the final model's weights, a GitLab CI/CD
  pipeline, Sphinx docs, and two handover sessions.

## Highlights

### Motion data format

There are two main ways to represent motion. The simplest is keypoints: the 3D
position of each joint, frame by frame. The other is rotations: how much each
joint turns relative to its parent, starting from the hips. Rotations keep
bone lengths fixed and can drive a real character's rig, so most motion
capture data uses them.

<Figure src={representations} alt="Four ways to draw the same pose. Three keypoint-based ones: a grey stick figure, a coloured stick figure and a body with marker dots. One rotation-based one: a body mesh with a skeleton and rotation axes at the shoulder." caption="Keypoint-based and rotation-based representations. From Zhu et al., Human Motion Generation: A Survey (2023)." />

The standard rotation-based body is [SMPL](https://smpl.is.tue.mpg.de/). For
each frame, it describes a person with three sets of numbers:

- **Pose (θ), 72 numbers:** a 3D rotation for each of 24 joints. The first
  joint is the pelvis, the root, which sets which way the whole body faces.
  Every other joint turns relative to its parent, so when the shoulder turns,
  the elbow and the hand go with it.
- **Shape (β), 10 numbers:** the body's build, such as height, weight and
  proportions, as a mix of 10 basic body shapes. All zeros is the average
  body.
- **Translation, 3 numbers:** where the pelvis is in the room.

From these, SMPL builds a mesh of 6,890 vertices and 13,776 triangles. Skinning
weights tie each vertex to the joints near it: a vertex at the elbow follows
mostly the elbow, and a little of the shoulder and the wrist. So the skin bends
smoothly when a joint turns. SMPL+H adds hands (52 joints in all), and SMPL-X
adds hands and a face.

<Figure src={bodies} alt="Two body meshes in a T-pose with their joints marked: SMPL in teal, and SMPL-X in pink, which has many more joints in the hands and face." caption="SMPL and SMPL-X, with their joints marked." />

The models don't train on SMPL parameters directly. They use the HumanML3D
representation: 263 numbers per frame, built from the 22 joint positions.

| Part | Numbers | What it captures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Root turning speed | 1 | How fast the body turns about the vertical axis |
| Root speed | 2 | How fast the hips move across the floor |
| Root height | 1 | How high the hips are |
| Joint positions | 63 | 21 joints, relative to the root |
| Joint rotations | 126 | 21 joints, as continuous 6D rotations |
| Joint velocities | 66 | How fast each of the 22 joints moves |
| Foot contacts | 4 | Whether each heel and toe touches the ground |

The root is stored as speeds, not positions, so a walk looks the same wherever
it starts. The foot contacts help the model avoid feet that slide.

<Figure src={tree} alt="A wireframe SMPL body in a T-pose with its kinematic tree drawn on top: the spine in blue, the left limbs in green and the right limbs in red, with numbered joints." caption="SMPL's kinematic tree. Each joint rotates relative to its parent, out from the pelvis." />

### Motion data processing

Raw motion capture can't go straight into a model. Every dataset records at
its own frame rate, with its own skeleton, and people stand wherever they
like. So motion data usually goes through a few stages before training:

| S/N | Stage | What it does | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Pose extraction | Runs the SMPL parameters through the body model to get the 3D joint positions, and resamples every clip to 20 frames a second. | Our datasets recorded at different rates: AIST++ at 60 fps, HumanML3D at 20. At one rate, a frame means the same time in every dataset. The models and the evaluator we reuse were built for HumanML3D at 20 fps. |
| 2 | Pose processing | Trims long clips, and adds a left-right mirror copy of every motion, with "left" and "right" swapped in its captions. | Dance clips run up to 48 seconds, while most HumanML3D clips last a few seconds. Mirroring doubles the data, and the model sees every move done with both sides of the body. |
| 3 | Normalisation and motion representation | Scales every body to one skeleton, puts the feet on the floor, moves the start to the centre and turns the first frame to face the same way. Then it builds the 263 numbers per frame. | The model should learn the movement, not the person's height, where they stood in the studio or which way they faced. |
| 4 | Train, validation and test split | 80% train, 5% validation, 15% test. | The test motions stay unseen, so the scores show how the model does on new motion, not how well it remembers. |
| 5 | Mean and variance | Works out the average and spread of each of the 263 numbers, from the training split only. | Angles, speeds and heights have very different scales. Normalising puts them on one scale, so no feature drowns out the others. The reference code used the whole dataset, which leaks the test set into training. A teammate spotted it, and we fixed it. |
| 6 | Text processing | Cleans each caption, tags its parts of speech, and matches it to its motion and the mirror copy. | Each motion must keep the right captions after renaming and mirroring. |
| 7 | Animate | Renders random samples as stick figures. | Numbers can't show a body lying on its side. This check is how we found AIST++ motions that weren't upright. |
| 8 | Aggregate | Merges several processed datasets into one. | One model can then train on more than one dataset, such as HumanML3D and AIST++ for dance. |

<Figure src={dataPipeline} zoom alt="The data processing pipeline. Motions go through pose extraction from SMPL parameters, mirroring, normalisation to a target skeleton facing Z+, and conversion to a 263-dimensional motion representation. Annotations go through text cleaning and part-of-speech tagging." caption="The data pipeline for motions (top) and captions (bottom)." />

### Training pipeline

A model can only do what its data shows it. We trained on three public
datasets, and each one taught the model something different:

| Dataset | What's in it | Size | Captions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [HumanML3D](https://github.com/EricGuo5513/HumanML3D) | Studio motion capture of daily activities (walking, jumping), sports (swimming, karate), acrobatics (cartwheels) and some dance. Clips last 7 seconds on average. SMPL+H body, 22 joints used. | 14,616 motions | 44,970, about 3 per motion |
| [AIST++](https://google.github.io/aistplusplus_dataset/) | Studio dance in 10 genres, such as ballet jazz, breaking, house and hip-hop. SMPL body with no shape, at 60 fps. | 1,408 dance clips | None. We wrote template captions from each clip's genre and move. |
| [Motion-X](https://motion-x-dataset.github.io/) | Whole-body motion with hands and face (SMPL-X), much of it taken from online video. We used 7 of its subsets: martial arts such as kung fu and tai chi, people playing instruments, acting, everyday actions and more. | 25,865 sequences, 51,370 with mirroring | Yes |

HumanML3D is the standard benchmark, so every published model trains and
scores on it. But it has little dance, and few moves outside a mocap studio.
AIST++ has the dance the studio wanted, without a single caption. Motion-X has
much more variety than both:

The team replicated four published models before choosing one. They take
quite different routes from text to motion:

| Model | How it generates | Our training time | Paper's |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [T2M](https://github.com/EricGuo5513/text-to-motion) | A variational autoencoder, one short snippet at a time, after predicting the motion's length | 16 h, 1 A100 | – |
| [MDM](https://github.com/GuyTevet/motion-diffusion-model) | Diffusion on the motion itself, with a transformer | 31 h, ⅓ A100 | 72 h, 1 RTX 2080 Ti |
| [MLD](https://github.com/ChenFengYe/motion-latent-diffusion) | Diffusion in a compressed latent space, after training an autoencoder | 228 h, ⅓ A100 | 12 h, 8 V100s |
| [T2M-GPT](https://github.com/Mael-zys/T2M-GPT) | Turns motion into tokens, then predicts the next token like a language model | about 3 days | 78 h |

MDM gave the best text match and trained in a day and a half on our share of
a GPU, so it became the base. MLD scored a little better on realism, but at
228 hours a run we could have trained it once, and MDM we could tune many
times. I replicated MDM with a teammate, then rebuilt its training inside our
framework, with Hydra configs, MLflow logging and unit tests.

MDM, the Motion Diffusion Model, is a diffusion model: the same family as
DALL·E 2 and Stable Diffusion, which make images. A diffusion model learns one
skill, which is to take a noisy motion and work out the clean motion under
the noise.

**Training.** Take a real motion from the dataset, say a person walking, as
its 263 numbers per frame. Pick a random step between 1 and 1,000, and add
that much Gaussian noise. At a low step the walk is still clear. At the last
step it's pure static. The model gets the noisy motion, the step number and
the caption, and has to predict the clean walk. Its loss is how far that
prediction is from the real motion. Over many thousands of examples, it learns
what real human motion looks like, and how each caption shapes it.

**Generating.** Start from pure noise, as long as the motion you want. The
model predicts a clean motion from it. We add back a little less noise, and
ask again. Each round the motion gets cleaner, until a walk comes out. The
paper used 1,000 rounds. We used the 50-step version, about 20 times faster
with comparable results.

Three choices make this work for motion:

- **A transformer instead of a U-Net.** Most image models use a U-Net. MDM
  treats each frame as a token, and attention lets every frame look at every
  other frame, which suits a sequence in time.
- **It predicts the clean motion, not the noise.** So the output at every step
  is a real motion, and the paper can add losses on joint positions, speeds
  and foot contact, which reduce sliding feet.
- **The text goes through CLIP.** A frozen CLIP text encoder turns the prompt
  into numbers. CLIP already knows many words and styles, so the model
  doesn't have to learn language from 44,970 captions alone.

<Figure src={mdm} zoom alt="Two diagrams. Left: a text prompt goes through a frozen CLIP text encoder, a random mask and a linear layer, and is added to the diffusion step t from an MLP to make one condition token. The noisy motion frames go through a linear layer, get a positional embedding, and pass with the token through a transformer encoder, which outputs a clean frame for each input frame. Right: sampling starts from Gaussian noise at step T. MDM predicts the clean motion, which is noised back to step T−1 and fed in again, until step 1 gives the final motion." caption="MDM. Left: one denoising step. Right: generating a motion, from noise to the final motion. From Tevet et al., Human Motion Diffusion Model (2023)." />

One more method, classifier-free guidance, makes the motion follow the text
more closely. In training, the caption is sometimes masked out (the "random
mask" in the diagram), so the same model also learns to move with no text at
all. When generating, the model runs twice at each step, with the caption and
without it. The difference between the two is what the text adds, and we push
the output further in that direction: 2.5 times as far in our setup. A higher
value follows the prompt more closely, but gives less variety.

To get the final model, the team tuned MDM one hyperparameter at a time, about
13 runs, each one more than a day long. The best mix (a lower learning rate,
more dropout and more attention heads) raised R-precision from 0.731 to 0.780
and brought FID from 0.569 down to 0.471, against MDM with its default
settings.

### Evaluation

I built the evaluation module, which generates a motion for each test caption
and scores it during training, logging to MLflow. Every score comes from a
pretrained pair of text and motion encoders that place captions and motions in
the same space:

| Metric | What it asks | Better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R-precision (top 3) | Is the motion's own caption among the 3 closest of 32? | Higher |
| FID | Does the spread of motions look like real motion capture? | Lower |
| Multimodal distance | How far is each motion from its caption? | Lower |
| Diversity | How different are motions for different captions? | Close to real data |
| Multimodality | How different are motions for the same caption? | Higher, within reason |

When I first connected the evaluation to training, the scores came out much
worse than expected. The same model scored 0.523 on R-precision during
training, and 0.739 when I evaluated it on its own. The team was about to tune
hyperparameters on these numbers, so I had to know which one was wrong.

I compared what the two paths did differently. The training-time path
generated motions without classifier-free guidance, so the model barely
followed the captions. I made both paths use guidance, the same as inference,
and added a warning when it's switched off. The model had been fine all
along. With the evaluator fixed, every tuning run after that was judged on
numbers we could trust.

The scores also move from run to run. Three evaluations of the same model gave
FID 0.557, 0.580 and 0.653. So we read small gaps with care, and trained the
final setup ten times to check that its result held.

### Fine-tuning

The studio wanted dance moves for in-game emotes, and HumanML3D has almost
none. We fine-tuned MDM on the 1,408 AIST++ dance clips, and it learned the
genres well: ask for ballet jazz and you get turns and travelling steps
instead of a shuffle.

Fine-tuning is as much art as science. AIST++ is a tenth the size of
HumanML3D, and the model began to forget what it knew. Prompts with no dance
in them started to dance:

<Clips caption="“A man walks forward, sits on the ground and crosses his legs”, before and after fine-tuning.">
  <Clip label="Before fine-tuning" src="/work/ai-singapore/sit-hml.mp4" poster="/work/ai-singapore/sit-hml.webp"
        alt="A stick figure lowers itself to the ground and crosses its legs." />
  <Clip label="After fine-tuning" src="/work/ai-singapore/sit-ft.mp4" poster="/work/ai-singapore/sit-ft.webp"
        alt="A stick figure stays on its feet and moves as if dancing." />
</Clips>

This is catastrophic forgetting: train on a narrow set, and the model drifts
from what it learned before. The usual remedies are to freeze the early
layers, so only the later ones adapt, and to mix some of the original data
back in. We added layer freezing to the trainer, and I found that the two
datasets need shared normalisation statistics, because the AIST++
statistics differ from HumanML3D's. These went to the studio as next steps,
along with a prototype that captions the dance clips with a video language
model, [VideoLLaMA3](https://github.com/DAMO-NLP-SG/VideoLLaMA3).

### CI/CD optimisations

I set up the GitLab pipeline: lint and unit tests, build the Docker image,
push it to Google Cloud, then a manual deploy to the GPU cluster. The first
version took 20 minutes a run. These changes brought it to 2:

| Change | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Micromamba on Alpine as the base image | A much smaller image than full conda, so it builds and pulls faster. |
| Cache the conda environment and pip downloads | Jobs reuse packages instead of installing them again. |
| `--disable-pip-version-check` and `--no-compile` | pip skips the version check and compiling bytecode at install time. |

## Reflections

### Prioritisation

In software, teams are encouraged to work in an agile way. On this project,
that meant:

- three-week sprints, nine of them plus a release sprint;
- a product backlog of user stories, ranked by our research partner at SUTD;
- sprint planning, with every task sized as one, three or five days;
- daily stand-ups;
- a sprint review every three weeks, where we showed our work to the studio
  and SUTD;
- retrospectives, to change how we worked;
- a minimum viable model as the target: the smallest model that proves the
  idea works.

It took me some practice to get into this mindset, because I have a
perfectionist streak. Early on, I went deep on small parts and polished
features the minimum viable model didn't need yet. In school, "agile" sounded like a buzzword. In
practice it means knowing what's good enough, failing fast and iterating
quickly. Rather than perfect one part, it matters more to understand what the
team and the product need at that moment: a working model for the next
review, a fix that unblocks a teammate, or numbers the studio can decide on.

That wasn't easy with diffusion models. One MDM training run took more than a
day, and MLD took more than nine. Every detour cost days, so I picked up "eat
the frog" from Brian Tracy: do the hardest, most important task first, before
the easy ones fill the day.

When I planned captions for the dance clips, I wrote down that a second step
with GPT would be overkill for now. I tried the video model on its own first,
and handed the rest to the studio as next steps.

What I do now:

- break the problem down and keep a priority list;
- eat the frog: the hardest or least certain task goes first;
- write down what done looks like before I start;
- get something working end to end, see where it fails, and improve that;
- when I catch myself polishing, ask whether it moves the metric or the
  deliverable. If it doesn't, it waits.

### Working with different people

Everyone is different. People come with different motivations and different
beliefs about how work should be done. Some want to push hard, some want to do
their part well, and some find too much structure stressful.

Under deadline pressure, one teammate got frustrated with the others' pace,
and the mood dipped. I met him one to one to understand what was frustrating
him. Then we put the focus on processes, not people. A complaint about a
person makes them defend themselves, and a process gives everyone a shared
problem to fix. So we added:

- a Thursday sync on how each person's part connected to the others;
- sprint retros, to raise what wasn't working without naming anyone;
- a daily update from me on what I'd done and what was next, which others
  started to post too.

The team delivered every
sprint on plan, and SUTD signed off before the release sprint was over.

It also changed how I lead. I used to push one way of working on everyone.
Now I watch how a team works first, let people own their part, and help the
team find its own balance. I step in more directly only when work stops
moving.

### Communication

My team's backgrounds were varied: two fresh graduates (me included), an
engineer with years of experience, and two career switchers. I spent a lot of
time explaining core computing fundamentals, like Linux and Git, and even more
complicated ideas, like diffusion models and how CLIP turns text into numbers.

I realised that when I couldn't find an analogy, or struggled to explain
something simply, it meant I didn't understand it well enough myself. It's
like when a child asks you a simple "why" question, and you find your own
understanding is being tested too. Explaining became a test of my own
understanding.

So I learned to pitch the same idea at different levels: the intuition for
someone new, the details for someone technical. I also wrote onboarding
guides, built a small interactive repo for practising Linux commands and
version control, and added checks to CI/CD, so the common mistakes were caught
before review instead of in it.
