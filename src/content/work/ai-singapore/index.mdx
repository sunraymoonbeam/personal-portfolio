## The project

Animating a game character takes a lot of manual work. Someone designs the
character, rigs it, then animates each movement by hand, and one character can
take weeks. At AI Singapore I was one of five apprentices on a seven-month
project with a game studio and SUTD: you type "a man walking" and get back a
walking animation, joints and mesh, ready to import into Unity. Think ChatGPT,
but for motion.

We delivered a framework, not only a model. One data pipeline takes motion
capture from several datasets, training pipelines plug in different models,
one evaluation module scores them all, and the whole thing ships as Docker
images with the final model's weights. We ran it as Scrum, with three-week
sprints and a review with the studio and SUTD at the end of each.

## Noteworthy projects

### One pipeline for every dataset

Each motion dataset stores the body its own way. HumanML3D has 14,616 motions
with 44,970 captions at 20 frames a second. AIST++ has 1,408 dance clips at
60, a different skeleton, no body shape and no captions at all. Motion-X
uses SMPL-X, which adds hands and a face.

I wrote the AIST++ conversion: fill the missing body shape with the average,
resample, and build captions from the clip's metadata, so "break, indian step"
becomes "A man is doing an indian step, Break style dance". Later I split the
processing into eight stages, added SMPL-X support for Motion-X, and replaced
a step that loaded every motion into memory to compute the mean and standard
deviation. It crashed on the bigger datasets. Welford's online algorithm gives
the same numbers one file at a time.

We also found a leak. The reference code computed those statistics over the
whole dataset, test set included. We moved to training-set statistics only, and
I retrained our models on the fixed data. The scores went down, and they were
honest.

### Training and an evaluation we could trust

We replicated several published models before choosing one, and I did the MDM
(Motion Diffusion Model) replication and its training pipeline. It's a
transformer that learns to remove noise from a motion one step at a time,
guided by the text. On a third of an A100 it trained in 31 hours, where the
paper used three days on a 2080 Ti. A latent-diffusion model we also tried took
228 hours, which settled that choice.

I built the evaluation module. It generates motions from the test captions and
scores them: R-precision (does the motion match its own caption better than 31
others?), FID (does it look like real motion?), plus diversity and
multimodality, logged to MLflow during training. Its first numbers inside
training were much worse than the standalone evaluator's. The model was fine.
My evaluator was sampling without classifier-free guidance, the setting that
makes the model follow the text. With it on, R-precision went from 0.523 to
0.739, matching the standalone run. I added a warning for when it's off.

With an evaluator we trusted, the team tuned the hyperparameters. The final
model scored 0.780 R-precision and 0.471 FID, against 0.707 and 0.489 for the
published MDM.

### Teaching it to dance

The studio wanted dance moves for in-game emotes, and the main dataset has
almost none. I fine-tuned MDM on the AIST++ dance clips, and it learned the
genres: ask for ballet jazz and you get turns.

It also forgot things. "A man walks forward, sits and crosses his legs" started
to dance. That's catastrophic forgetting: train on a narrow set and the model
drifts from what it knew. I tried freezing layers and combining the two
datasets' statistics. The fine-tuned model is better at dance and worse at
everything else, and that trade-off is what I presented to the studio.

The captions were the other gap. AIST++ only has metadata, so I prototyped
captioning the 1,408 dance videos with a video language model, VideoLLaMA3:
eight prompts, a Docker image to run it, and a plan to merge several captions
into one with GPT-4o. We handed it over as the next step.

### CI/CD and deployment

I set up our GitLab pipeline: lint and unit tests, build the Docker image,
push it to Google Cloud, then a manual deploy to the GPU cluster. The first
version took 20 minutes a run. A Micromamba image on Alpine, caching for conda
and pip, and quieter pip flags brought it to 2.

<Figure src={gitlab} alt="A GitLab pipeline: test:pytest and test:quality pass, then build:docker and deploy:docker pass, and a manual deploy:runai job waits." caption="Test, build, deploy. The last step waits for a person." />

The studio got the final system as a Docker Compose setup with a training
service and an inference service, a FastAPI endpoint, the model weights, and
data pulled with DVC. We ran two handover sessions to walk their engineers
through the code.

## Reflections

### Data first

The biggest jumps in quality came from fixing the data: one consistent format,
statistics without the test set, captions for clips that had none. You can't
build good AI models without good data. If I did it again, I'd start there
before touching the model.

### Doubting the numbers

When the evaluation said the model was bad, the easy move was to start tuning.
The evaluator was wrong, not the model. Now when a number surprises me, I check
how it was measured before I act on it.

### Knowing what's good enough

Early on I spent time polishing parts of the MVP that didn't need it yet. On a
proof of concept with a deadline, that time belongs to the riskiest part. My
manager's advice was to know what's good enough and fail fast, and I still
use it.

### Explaining it simply

My team had a fresh graduate, an experienced engineer and two career
switchers. Explaining Linux and Git to people from other fields took more time
than I expected, and it showed me where my own understanding was thin. I wrote
onboarding guides, built a small interactive repo for practising Linux commands
and version control, and added checks to CI/CD so fewer mistakes reached
review.

### Processes, not people

When a teammate was frustrated and the mood dipped, I talked to him one to one
and suggested a Thursday sync so everyone knew how their part touched the
others. We talked about the process, never about who was slow. It didn't fix
everything, and I learned where my job ended: keep my own work on track,
protect my energy, and raise it with the manager when it's needed. We still
hit our deadlines.
