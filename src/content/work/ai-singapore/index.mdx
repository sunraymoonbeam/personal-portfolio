## The project

Animating a game character takes a lot of manual work. Someone designs the
character, rigs it, then animates each movement by hand. At AI Singapore I
worked on an MVP for a gaming company that skips the last step: you type
"a man walking" and get back a walking animation, joints and mesh, ready to
import into Unity.

## What I built

Our motion capture data came from several sources, and each one stored joint
positions, kinematics and velocities its own way. I built a modular pipeline
that converts all of it into one training format, then trained diffusion
models on it in PyTorch.

Some clips had missing or thin text descriptions. We ran a multimodal video LLM
over those clips to write new ones, and that improved our text-to-motion
metrics by 20% over baseline.

I also optimised our GitLab CI/CD pipelines. Testing and deployment now take a
fifth of the time they used to.

## Looking back

The biggest jumps in quality came from fixing the data: one consistent format,
and descriptions for the clips that had none. If I did it again, I'd start
there before touching the model.

My team had a fresh graduate, an experienced engineer and two career switchers.
Explaining Linux and Git to people from other fields took more time than I
expected, and it showed me where my own understanding was thin. I wrote
onboarding guides, built a small interactive repo for practising Linux commands
and version control, and added checks to CI/CD so fewer mistakes reached
review.

I also learned when to stop. Early on I spent time polishing parts of the MVP
that didn't need it yet. On a proof of concept with a deadline, that time
belongs to the riskiest part.
