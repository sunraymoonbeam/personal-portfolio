I started climbing in 2018 through a PE elective in school. I'd never really
thought about it before, but the first time I got on the wall, something about
it felt strangely natural.

Climbing is so much fun because no two climbs are the same. There are endless
ways to set a route, and different styles to climb it in: bouldering on short
walls with no rope, or sport climbing up longer routes on one.

The holds change everything. A crimp takes only your fingertips, a jug fits
your whole hand, a sloper gives you nothing but friction, and a pinch has to be
squeezed between thumb and fingers. So do the body positions: a heel hook, a
drop knee, a flag to keep your balance. Every climb asks for a different mix
of body awareness, reading the moves before you make them, strength,
flexibility, a clear head and some guts.

Two years after I started, I had a really bad fall and broke both my arms.
Coming back took time, but I didn't want to give up a sport that had given me
so much.

Since then, climbing has taken me around the world and introduced me to some
of my closest friends.

What keeps me coming back is the problem solving. A move can feel impossible
for a whole session, and then I turn my hip slightly, move a foot a few
centimetres or grip the same hold from a different angle, and it suddenly goes.
Trying harder rarely fixes it.

I think a lot of life works the same way. Something small, changed in the right
place, can have a large impact.

For a long time I was a grade chaser. I wanted to send harder and harder
grades, and every session was a test of whether I'd moved up. It's good never
to be fully satisfied, but somewhere along the way I realised I wasn't really
having fun any more. I'd stopped enjoying the movement itself.

Not every session has to be a step up. Knowing how to simply enjoy something
is a skill too, a rarer one than it sounds, and like any skill it takes practice.
