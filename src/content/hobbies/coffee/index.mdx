For most of my life, coffee meant kopi. Then I got curious and bought a V60.
My first cup tasted like blueberries. I didn't know coffee could taste like
blueberries.

<Photos>
  <Shot src={cover} alt="A V60 dripper, server and hand grinder on a kitchen bench by a window." caption="My setup." />
</Photos>

Since then, what should have been a simple morning drink has turned into a
small daily experiment. It's also a ritual, and a big part of why I look
forward to waking up.

The fun part is how many things change the cup. I tweak one, taste what
changes, then try to work out why it changed. These are the ones I play with:

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Factor</th><th>What it changes</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>The beans</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>Where they grew and how they were processed decide what the cup can taste like at all.</li>
          <li>Washed: the fruit comes off before the beans dry. Clean and bright, with clear acidity.</li>
          <li>Natural: the beans dry inside the whole cherry. Fruity, heavier, sometimes funky.</li>
          <li>Honey: some of the sticky fruit stays on while they dry. In between: sweet, with a rounder body.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>The roast</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>Light roasts keep more of the bean's own flavours, but they're denser and harder to extract.</li>
          <li>Dark roasts taste more of the roast, and turn bitter if you push them.</li>
          <li>Beans taste best a week or two after roasting.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Grind size</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>Finer grounds extract more and slow the water down.</li>
          <li>Too fine tastes bitter and dry. Too coarse tastes sour and thin.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Coffee-to-water ratio</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>Sets the strength.</li>
          <li>About 1 g of coffee to 16 g of water is a common place to start.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Water temperature</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>Hotter water extracts more.</li>
          <li>Most recipes sit between 90 and 96°C: hotter for light roasts, cooler for dark ones.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>The water itself</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>Minerals carry the flavour out.</li>
          <li>Very soft water tastes flat, and very hard water tastes chalky.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Brewing method</td>
      <td>
        <ul>
          <li>A pour-over like the V60 runs water through the coffee and a paper filter, for a clean, light cup.</li>
          <li>Immersion, like the AeroPress, steeps everything together, for a heavier body.</li>
        </ul>
      </td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Since the V60 I've tried the AeroPress, immersion brewing, cold brew and a few
other ways of making filter coffee. Espresso would be the next rabbit hole.

I've tried plenty of other recipes too: coffee mixed with tea (with black
oolong, it's amazing), dirty matcha, which is a matcha latte with a shot of
espresso, and my infamous Ferrero Rocher cold brew.

In my spare time I also love drinking coffee around the world, from specialty
cafes to chai in the hills of northern India.

benchmark rather high. Until I get close, I'll keep changing the numbers every
morning.
