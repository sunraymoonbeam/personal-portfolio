Some things travel unusually well between cultures. You do not need to speak a
language to enjoy its music. You do not need to grow up somewhere to understand
that its food tastes good.

When I was younger, though, I was much more interested in food that felt
foreign to me. Growing up in Singapore, Western food seemed more exciting
precisely because it was different.

<Figure src={clams} alt="Linguine in a pot, clams in another, and garlic softening in a pan." caption="The Western food era. I stand by the clams." />

I think getting older did the opposite.

I started appreciating Asian food more, and especially the food I grew up
around. These days I cook quite a lot for my family, and I have slowly worked
my way through dishes like char kway teow, Hokkien mee, prawn mee and chicken
rice.

<Figure src={mise} alt="A kitchen counter covered in bowls of prepared prawns, pork and chicken beside sauces and a knife block." caption="The part nobody photographs, and the part that decides how dinner goes." />

Chicken rice is one of my favourites because it looks deceptively simple.

It is chicken. It is rice.

Unfortunately, neither part is actually that simple.

The rice needs aromatics and chicken fat, so first you need the fat. The stock
needs enough flavour to carry the whole plate. The chicken has its own
problems: getting the meat cooked properly while keeping the skin soft and
gelatinous, then cutting the thing cleanly enough that it still resembles food
when it reaches the table.

There are a surprising number of decisions hiding inside a plate that
disappears in ten minutes.

And I think that is part of why I like making local food. When you finally get
it right, you are not really making something novel. You are recreating
something familiar.

There is something very comforting about being able to make food at home that
tastes like home.
